# Layout craft: a rulebook that is serious and beautiful and still fast at the table

## 2026-11-16: for the book's look (the Action cards' "book feel")

**The question.** What do RPG layout designers, information designers and experienced reviewers say about laying out rules and ability blocks so that a book is both beautiful / serious and fast to use at the table? Covered: layout designers on their craft; atmosphere against usability; bold, italics, small caps and color in rules text; color coding and color blindness; icons against words; accessibility and readability facts; HTML rulebooks and SRD sites; designers' checklists for a block. `card-layout.md` already holds where the facts of a block sit (the roll line, grid against stacked lines): not repeated here.

**How far to trust this file.** One pass, by an agent (about 60 steps); not re-checked by a second. There is no raw report: everything found is here.

## Q1. RPG layout designers on their craft

**Explorers Design (Clayton Notestine)** [checked: each essay read in full on its page, with its date].
- *General Design 101* (Oct 2024): "Test your work by asking 'why?' [...] Have an answer that can be tied back to your project's big idea"; decisions add either "order, harmony, and affinity" or "chaos, tension, and contrast"; "order makes your design approachable and chaos makes it fresh". "Rules are relative. There's usually a path forward that doesn't require abandoning your creative concept or a design's usability."
- *Typography 101* (Nov 2024), adapted from Butterick: "Body Text. It's the foundation of every good book [...] start here." Size "10–12 points in print. Digitally, it's 15–25 pixels. In games, we tend to make our type too small or too big. When in doubt, compare your text to non-rpg books." Leading "120–145% of the point size". Line length "45–75 characters per line [...] in rpgs, our text is more mechanical and often benefits from paragraph breaks." "Your body text should be easy on the eyes, easy to read, and not too flashy. Let your game stand out with its mechanics, writing, and art. Don't make the text extreme to compensate." Tip: "Consider using one type family [...] you can focus on contrast by modifying the size and weight of your elements." The "Nohr Method": a grid of the same 800-character passage at every size and line height, printed, read, narrowed.
- *Mörk Borg (Layout Exhibit)* (May 2024): "Many describe it as chaotic, but our goal is to dispel that notion." "When in doubt, the next relevant piece of information is always to the right or below where your eye is." "Common regions [...] When multiple elements share a common region, they're perceived (and read) as a group." "There's a limit to how much deconstruction [...] an audience can take [...] This book has a grid system—it just happens to be after the rules and setting explanation." "Things like repetition, predictable rhythms, and patterns create stability, trust, and confidence in the reader. It's one of the core tenets of usability design". "space conveys information."
- *How to Design Signals in Books* (Dec 2025, from Jan V. White's *Editing by Design*): signals give "Distinction", "Orientation" ("Where am I?") and "Navigation". "Beware of false signals. Readers subconsciously look for patterns and can misinterpret decorations for signaling [...] if there is no meaning, it will confuse and even mislead your audience." "Use shared language between signals [...] Use identical words." "Design signals to match the visual identity [...] Use page numbers to reinforce your look and feel."
- *How to Design Lists in RPGs* (May 2026): a list "is 'loud'—designed to stand out". "Make items in the list structurally parallel." "attention-grabbing formatting is limited to the start of each successive bullet. Bold words in the middle of lists can make them harder to read." "For complicated lists, consider using lead-ins [...] with a bolder formatting. Think of your list like a series of ledes". Bullets "somewhere between 50% and 30% as big as the capital letter." Indent "one em".
- *False Dichotomies of RPG Design* (Apr 2025): "'Is your game hyper-usable or fun to read?' Well-written games can be fun to read and be usable." On white space: "It doesn't have to be filled with anything. Sometimes the emptiness is the point."

**Sean McCoy (Mothership), "Eleven Questions About Layout in RPGs"** [checked, his blog, Mar 2019]. "The person I imagine when I design a module is ordering pizza, answering questions about character creation, short on prep time, and waiting for half the group to show up." "Does white space matter? For a comfortable reading experience, absolutely. But we're not trying to design a comfortable reading experience we're trying to design something that someone can use while two players argue". "Can this text be bullet points? Can these bullet points be a table? Can this table be a diagram? Can this diagram be an illustration?" His list: "Do work in spreads / Don't make me flip the page unless I'm completely moving on from this section / Don't split paragraphs between columns / Don't make me look for the monster's stat line / Do give page numbers for every reference / Don't make me read lore to get to the game / Do make use of the physical parts of the book that are easiest to get to (e.g. the endpapers, back cover [...])". "If people have a complaint about Mothership in the layout department it's that the text is too small or that there's white text on black [...] The biggest rule is: make it useful or beautiful." On genre: "In fantasy the most common UI theme is illuminated manuscripts and tomes. Everyone wants things to look like old books, because that's all we've got." Resist "a template [...] flow your text into it like a novel".

**Gavin Norman, Old-School Essentials** [checked, his blog, Aug 2019]. "When I refer to 'the Old-School Essentials style', I'm not referring to the visual design of the books (e.g. the choice of fonts, table colours, etc.). Rather, I'm referring to the design principles that I used when writing, editing, and laying out the text. One of the most important OSE design principles is that each piece of content should fit onto a single page or spread, for the sake of usability [...] the reader will never have to flip a page back and forth". The spread also "serves as a limitation on class complexity".

**Paizo on Pathfinder 2e's playtest block** [checked, Paizo blog "Attack the Stat Block" and the forum poll, May 2018]. The block is ordered by when it is read: "you're looking at the middle section when it's not the monster's turn, and at the bottom section on its turn"; a reaction sits "down here because the trigger is most likely to occur during the redcap's own turn." Erik Mona (publisher), in the poll thread: "I think even in-house we are skeptical that color is a useful way to delineate information, so that one is really on the fence [...] The symbols are a different story. We think they'll really help [...] Accessibility is a VERY important issue to us [...] the PRD and all 'text-based' versions of the rules will have the [A] and [R] designations in plain text".

**Other working designers** [checked, each on its page with its date].
- Graham Walmsley ("Doing simple layout", Aug 2023): "If there's one thing that makes a game look professional, it's a good typeface [...] Instead, use a classic typeface"; he warns against genre fonts ("a bad 'futuristic' typeface"). Margins as simple fractions of the page; paragraph spacing a fraction or multiple of the type size; a footnote credits Jason Morningstar: "x-height matters more than the size itself."
- Golem Productions (Sabrina Jatscha, a graphic designer, Mar 2025): "There is one layout rule that stands above all else: It must be easy to use at the table!"; they aim "to fit all the necessary information to run it on a single spread." Their first draft "was essentially just text—no fonts, no colors, no highlights".
- James Maliszewski (Grognardia, Feb 2026): "Maybe the trick is not to aim for either 'professional' or 'primitive,' but for appropriate. A roleplaying book should be clear, usable, and inviting. It should look like it was produced for play, not for display." "Bad layout, illegible type, muddy photocopies, and willfully crude artwork are not inherently virtuous [...] they just make it harder to read."
- Welcome to the Deathtrap (Brian Rideout, Aug 2021), on adventures: "Hail the Mighty Spread [...] avoid making the reader have to flip pages, especially in the middle of a paragraph"; "a good layout and information design can vastly improve the play experience. Especially if not overdone."
- **Brennen Reece** (Bully Pulpit Games' designer, interviewed Mar 2017) [checked]: "RPGs are first teaching texts, and then reference texts, so I'd start there. There are some incredibly well designed books for art instruction, bushcraft, cooking, woodworking, knitting". On the 1983 Red Box: "There aren't any weird typefaces, obnoxious fake parchment page backgrounds, eldritch page borders. The typography is pretty damned competent." His "mid-century formalism": "clean, crisp typography, stripped of ornamentation [...] concerned with communicating information rather than decorating it." From Bringhurst: "the design should honor the text, and the reading experience shouldn't be made more difficult by poor craftsmanship and visual distractions"; designers "use a very small palette of typefaces". From Tufte: "Anything that isn't contributing to the signal is contributing to the noise." "I have no problem suggesting an edit to fit the text on the page."
- **The Gauntlet forum, "How To Make RPG Books More Readable?"** (Apr 2019, an archived copy) [checked]: two-page spreads ("I don't want to have to flip pages to continue reading something"), "a really good index", PDF links "to find the definition of a move". "White space. Most RPG pages are far too crowded"; "Concision. Cut the text in half"; "Since the books serves at least two masters (learning and reference) a perfect organization is probably impossible. So repeat necessary text rather than reference it." Another: "any game text faces a dilemma between two objectives [...] The aim to teach the game and the aim to support play [...] There is a reason that Fantasy Flight is providing two rulebooks with many of their board games". A designer's list: "A clear visual hierarchy [...] Simplicity before graphics effects [...] Be consistent in your layout and design."
- **The other side: "sterile" and "art book"** [checked]. Ryan Howard (RGH Press, Mar 2024): "Move too far into the realm of informational presentation and you have a text book or something sterile like Old-School Essentials. Move too far the other way and you have something like [...] Cy_Borg which has completely sacrificed legibility for the sake of the aesthetic"; "A proper RPG book should be designed to be useful. It is a reference book at the end of the day"; his model is the DK Eyewitness book (a central image, a lead paragraph, labeled details). Malmuria (Familiar Waves, Aug 2022): OSE "is obviously the standard bearer [...] While some say it lacks the charm of the original, it more than makes up for it in efficient, 'control panel' two page spreads"; Mörk Borg's "layout and art in the core book conveys its tone non-verbally [...] the parts that need to be well organized are, exceptionally so". Orthopraxy (Jan 2026), answering Ben Milton's video against Goodman Games' dense layout: Winter's Daughter "was written not for the reader, but for the active GM"; a novel-like module can be "eaten" and remembered so the book is not needed at the table.
- **Johan Nohr** (Mörk Borg) [checked, Grognardia's interview, Dec 2020]: "we basically wanted to see what would happen if we broke a lot of rules and challenged a lot of norms when it comes to book design [...] It's still a fully playable game, mind you, but the art aspect is important." Explorers Design notes "a bare-bones edition of Mörk Borg for people with accessibility difficulties" [read].
- **EN World, "Graphic Design Layout Artists: The Reason TTRPGs Look Great"** (Jul 2024) [checked]: a layout artist quoted in the article: "I shouldn't have to dismantle your work completely to uncover stats [...] It's incredibly vexing to find myself resorting to using a highlighter". Replies: "having black background makes things harder to read [...] I might be less inclined to purchase"; "White background, great black and white art, readable font and I am happy"; "attractive layouts are nice, useful layouts are necessary [...] Information architecture is insanely important"; "Even very structured design can still have a ton of character."
- **Draw Steel's book** [checked, EN World, Jun 2026]: against: "the lack of modern layouting tools like boxes, highlighting etc. It feels like this was just a word document made to PDF". For: "the issues with layout are overblown -- at east as they relate to class layout. It COULD use a little color or other design flourishes, but the abilities are actually pretty well laid out in practice"; "The rules portion where it's big blocks of text is where I struggle!"; "The text is smaller than necessary". Gaming Trend's review (2025) [checked]: the keyword definitions are "essential stuff, but it is presented rather dryly"; "a whole lot of book flipping"; one tab color for the whole classes third of the book "feels like a missed chance". No designer's own account of the ability block's look was found.

## Q2. Atmosphere against speed: how the sources settle it

- **Two jobs, said by many:** "RPGs are first teaching texts, and then reference texts" (Reece); "the books serves at least two masters (learning and reference)" (the Gauntlet); "The aim to teach the game and the aim to support play" (same thread), answered in board games by two books, a learn-to-play and a reference. "A reference book at the end of the day" (RGH Press). The opposite case: a novel-like text can be "eaten" and run from memory (Orthopraxy). [checked]
- **Where the two fight, the sources give the reference job the content and the atmosphere the frame.** OSE keeps "the visual design of the books" apart from its principles (one item a spread). Mörk Borg is chaotic in its setting pages and gridded where it is consulted: "This book has a grid system—it just happens to be after the rules"; "the parts that need to be well organized are, exceptionally so" (Notestine; Familiar Waves). Notestine: "Let your game stand out with its mechanics, writing, and art. Don't make the text extreme to compensate." [checked]
- **Mood through type, not through decoration.** Reece and Walmsley both send the designer to a period's real books and to "classic" faces, never to genre fonts or "obnoxious fake parchment page backgrounds, eldritch page borders"; Walmsley built *Stealing Cthulhu* on "a prayer book from around 1910". Notestine: look at "non-rpg books", "old books and catalogs". McCoy: fantasy's default look is "illuminated manuscripts and tomes". [checked]
- **What clutter is, in their words:** decoration a reader takes for meaning ("false signals"); spot art that fills space ("It doesn't have to be filled with anything"); bold in the middle of a list item; "Anything that isn't contributing to the signal is contributing to the noise" (Reece after Tufte); boxes inside regions that "confuse the order of operations" (Notestine on nested regions). [checked]
- **What makes an entry findable:** the same anatomy every time ("repetition, predictable rhythms, and patterns create stability, trust, and confidence"); a common region (a box, a rule, or space) that groups an entry; the next fact always right or below; identical words between an index or table of contents and the heading; an entry never split across a page turn. [checked]
- **The recurring complaints against the art pole:** white text on black (McCoy owns it as Mothership's main criticism; EN World: "having black background makes things harder to read"); text too small (Mothership; Draw Steel); "sacrificed legibility for the sake of the aesthetic" (RGH on Cy_Borg). **Against the sterile pole:** "sterile like Old-School Essentials" (RGH); "lacks the charm of the original" (reported by Familiar Waves); Draw Steel "feels like [...] a word document made to PDF" (EN World). [checked]

## Q3. Bold, italics, capitals, small caps in rules text

- **Butterick** [read]: "Bold or italic—think of them as mutually exclusive. That is rule #1. Rule #2: use bold and italic as little as possible. They are tools for emphasis. But if everything is emphasized, then nothing is emphasized." "With a serif font, use italic for gentle emphasis, or bold for heavier emphasis. If you're using a sans serif font, skip italic and use bold". All caps: "Fine for less than one line [...] suitable for headings shorter than one line [...] or other labels [...] Always add letterspacing to caps". Small caps: "very useful as an alternative to bold or italic or all caps", but use real ones ("ersatz small caps that word processors and web browsers generate" are "too tall, and their vertical strokes are too light"), "sparingly, add letterspacing".
- **The BDA** [read]: "Avoid underlining and italics [...] Use bold for emphasis"; no all caps for continuous text. Rello and Baeza-Yates [quoted]: italic versions "proved to be harder to read".
- **Notestine on lists** [checked]: "attention-grabbing formatting is limited to the start of each successive bullet. Bold words in the middle of lists can make them harder to read"; a bold lead-in turns each item into a "lede".
- **D&D 2024 capitalizes its game terms** [read, a fan's style guide (Blog of Holding) that lists them]: capitalized, among many, "Advantage/Disadvantage", "Bonus Action", conditions ("Grappled"), areas ("Emanation"), damage types ("Piercing"), "Hit Points", "Opportunity Attack", weapon properties ("Finesse"); left lowercase: "action", "attack roll", "damage roll", "saving throw", "turn". The reaction [checked, D&D Beyond forum, Nov 2023, on the playtest's "Prone condition"]: for: "while 'having the prone condition' is indeed ungainly, it's also incredibly clear"; "We've had nine years of seeing what that ['natural language'] has gotten us - endless misunderstandings"; against: "I'm less okay with the capitalization of basically every game concept. It makes sentences weirder to read"; "capitalisation is a horrible way to distinguish keywords, I'd prefer bold text or similar [...] Plus capitalisation is only going to cause confusion with the start of sentences, and names".
- **How the reference sites mark things** [read, the pages' HTML]: the 5e SRD site bolds only field labels ("Casting Time:", "Range:", "At Higher Levels.") and sets the spell's school line in italic; Archives of Nethys prints each Pathfinder 2e trait as its own boxed, linked word in a row under the name; the Steel Compendium prints Keywords / Distance / Target as labeled rows, without bold inside the effect text. 5e's convention of italic spell names in running text and Draw Steel's bold in printed effects were not checked [lead].
- No source was found that measures a book with every keyword colored; the one designer statement on color for rules text is Paizo's publisher's skepticism (Q4).

## Q4. Color coding: frequency, rarity, class; color blindness; print

- **4e's frequency colors** [quoted, Dave Noonan's blog via EN World, 2008]: "color conveys meaning in the graphic design. Specifically, at-will powers get green headers, encounter powers get red, and dailies get black." The header also printed the word (At-Will, Encounter, Daily) [lead: memory]. A fan making 4e cards on the same thread: "I would also suggest adding recognizable patterns to the colored backgrounds [...] Pittsburgh is overrun by colorblind gamers."
- **Paizo, 2018** [checked]: "even in-house we are skeptical that color is a useful way to delineate information [...] I suspect we'll be finding another way to handle the way it is used in the final book". Pathfinder 2e's printed books color only a few trait boxes (rarity, size, alignment in the original printing) [lead: memory, not checked].
- **The red-green pair is the trap**: green against red is exactly the most common deficiency's confusion (NEI [read]: "hard to tell the difference between red and green"), about 1 man in 12. The BDA [read]: "Avoid green and red/pink". WCAG 1.4.1 [read]: color is never "the only visual means of conveying information". WCAG's understanding page: "This should not in any way discourage the use of color on a page, or even color coding if it is complemented by other visual indication"; two colors that also differ in lightness count as a second cue: "a light green and a dark red [...] would pass if the contrast ratio is at least 3:1."
- **Double-coding and its limit** [checked, Colorblind Games, Apr 2023]: "A common and useful method called 'double-coding' [...] background patterns [...] symbols and colors"; but "Double-coding is awesome, but it also maintains the unequal challenge [...] by requiring re-learning with each new game." A proposed universal code argues against an icon per color: "learning all the symbols will become a big and complex task". League of Gamemakers (Jul 2014) [checked]: icons told apart by "simple shapes (square, circle, triangle) and very distinct colors". The same principle in a book is a printed word next to the color.
- **Print and one accent:** no RPG source on the cost of color or a black-and-white fallback was read. The nearest: Tufte CSS [read]: "We stick to the greyscale for text, reserving color for specific, careful use in figures and images"; EN World's reader: "White background, great black and white art, readable font and I am happy" [checked]; Draw Steel's chapter tabs in one color for the whole classes third "feels like a missed chance" [checked] (color used for orientation, not inside the text).

## Q5. Icons against words

- **Paizo's action glyphs** [checked, the 2018 poll thread]: the publisher: "The action symbols we're using are fairly large and not very plentiful in terms of variety [...] the PRD and all 'text-based' versions of the rules will have the [A] and [R] designations in plain text, which should help with ereaders". Readers: "in moderation they will be helpful, but it doesn't take many icons to turn something into a mess"; 4e's few icons (melee, ranged, close, area, a basic-attack ring) were "not particularly problematic" while AD&D 2e's thirteen school pictures were "relatively busy, didn't really reflect the school's theme, and there were too many of them"; "I copy/paste rules text with some frequency, and icons are much more likely to be lost"; road signs work because they "have three things that are used as identifiers: color, shape, and imagery/wording"; "I am literate not iconerated."
- **League of Gamemakers** [checked, Jul 2014]: an icon language must be "unique to the setting [...] and being recognizable"; "If the concepts are too complex and you're having to mash up your icons in weird ways [...] consider using text instead of icons"; "Avoid getting too detailed, especially if the icon is going to be small". Even an odd icon "can be learned if used sparingly".
- **The BDA** [read]: "Pictograms and graphics can help to locate and support information in the text" (to locate, beside the text, not in place of it).
- Gloomhaven's card icons and any study timing icon against word lookups: not found.

## Q6. Accessibility and readability: what the sources actually say

- **British Dyslexia Association, *Dyslexia friendly style guide*** [read, a copy of the BDA page (the 2023 guide) kept by Boston University; the BDA's own page loaded without its text]. Fonts: "Use sans serif fonts, such as Arial and Comic Sans [...] Alternatives include Verdana, Tahoma, Century Gothic, Trebuchet, Calibri, Open Sans." Size "12-14 point or equivalent (e.g. 1-1.2em / 16-19 px)". Line spacing "1.5/150% is preferable." "Avoid underlining and italics as this can make the text appear to run together and cause crowding. Use bold for emphasis." "Avoid using all capital letters [...] for continuous text." Headings "at least 20% larger than the normal text." Colour: "Use dark coloured text on a light (not white) background"; "Avoid green and red/pink"; "Use cream or a soft pastel colour"; "use matt paper rather than gloss". Layout: "Left align text, without justification"; "Avoid multiple columns (as used in newspapers)"; "Write short simple sentences: 60 to 70 characters is optimal" (the guide puts the number on sentences, not line length); "Use white space to remove clutter near text and group related content." "Pictograms and graphics can help to locate and support information in the text."
- **Serif against sans: the controlled study finds no difference at reading sizes.** Arditi and Cho, *Serifs and font legibility*, Vision Research 2005 [read]: "RSVP and continuous reading speeds showed no effect of serifs. When text is small or distant, serifs may, then, produce a tiny legibility increase due to the concomitant increase in spacing. However, our data exhibited no difference in legibility between typefaces that differ only in the presence or absence of serifs." "unlikely to be of significance at typical print sizes viewed under normal conditions." Their own caveats: one font of their own design, a small sample.
- **Dyslexia and fonts** [quoted: Rello and Baeza-Yates, *Good fonts for dyslexia*, ASSETS 2013, 48 readers with dyslexia, eye-tracking; read through BYU's Editing Research summary, May 2020]: "special fonts like OpenDyslexic [...] were not, in fact, easier to read, but fonts like Arial and Helvetica were. Helvetica and Courier specifically led to shorter fixation durations"; "italics versions of these fonts proved to be harder to read". The paper itself was not opened.
- **WCAG 2.2** [read]. 1.4.1 Use of Color: "Color is not used as the only visual means of conveying information, indicating an action, prompting a response, or distinguishing a visual element." 1.4.3 Contrast (Minimum): "at least 4.5:1", "Large-scale text [...] at least 3:1"; "18 point text or 14 point bold text is judged to be large".
- **Color vision deficiency** [read, the US National Eye Institute]: "About 1 in 12 men have color vision deficiency"; "The most common type [...] makes it hard to tell the difference between red and green. Another type makes blue and yellow look the same." (1 in 12 is the "about 8%".)
- **Body size, leading, measure** [read, Butterick]: print body "10–12 point. On the web, 15–25 pixels"; line spacing "120–145% of the point size"; line length "45–90 characters [...] or 2–3 lowercase alphabets" ("Shorter lines are more comfortable to read [...] your eye has to travel farther from the end of one line to the beginning of the next"). Notestine narrows it to 45–75 for RPGs [checked]. "The typographic quality of your document is determined largely by how the body text looks [...] So start every project by making the body text look good."
- **Justified text** [read, Butterick]: "a matter of personal preference. It is not a signifier of professional typography [...] Books, on the other hand, tend to be justified"; "the justification engine of a word processor or web browser is rudimentary [...] So if I'm making a word-processor document or web page, I'll always left-align the text"; justify only with hyphenation on. The BDA: left-aligned, not justified [read].
- **Dark and light** [checked, NN/g, Feb 2020, summarizing Piepenbrock and others; the papers themselves not opened]: "In people with normal vision [...] visual performance tends to be better with light mode, whereas some people with cataract and related disorders may perform better with dark mode"; "the positive-polarity advantage increased linearly as the font size was decreased"; no difference in fatigue was found; NN/g does not recommend dark by default for a general audience but "strongly recommend[s] that designers allow users to switch". Tufte CSS uses "#fffff8 and #111111 because they are nearly indistinguishable from their 'pure' cousins, but dial down the harsh contrast" [read].
- **Myths to drop**: "serif for print, sans for screens" has no support at reading sizes (Arditi and Cho); "dyslexia fonts" did not help in Rello's study; a cream background is a BDA preference, not a measured finding in anything read here.

## Q7. HTML rulebooks and SRD sites

- **What the reference sites use** [read, their pages and stylesheets, Nov 2026]: Archives of Nethys: a sans (Lato, or Century Gothic in another theme), traits as linked boxes; the Steel Compendium: a documentation theme (Material for MkDocs, Roboto), the four facts as labeled rows; the Cairn 2e SRD: the "Just the Docs" documentation theme; the 5e SRD site (5thsrd.org): a serif stack (Constantia, Lucida Bright, Georgia), bold field labels; the Blades in the Dark SRD: a slab serif (Zilla Slab). Most are documentation templates: fast to search, not made to feel like a book. None of these sites was found to have a published design rationale.
- **What HTML adds that print can't** [checked]: every defined term can be a link to its definition (AoN's traits; the Gauntlet: PDF links "to find the definition of a move"; Notestine's *Signals* essay: "the underrated hyperlinks live in your pages' running headers. Use them to create links between the table of contents, section intros, and index"; his *How to Hyperlink*, Jul 2026, was fetched but not read).
- **Book feel on a screen, by those who build it** [read]: Tufte CSS: "simplicity, extensive use of sidenotes, tight integration of graphics with text, and carefully chosen typography"; a serif text face (ETBook, falling back to Palatino and Georgia); sections opened by "a bit of vertical space, a non-indented paragraph, and the first few words of the sentence set in small caps"; color kept out of the text; sidenotes that "stay out of the way" and fold away on small screens. Butterick's own book is a web book on the same principles (one text face, generous measure, no color in the body). Lancer's COMP/CON and Ironsworn's sites were not opened.

## Q8. Checklists for a block, as designers wrote them

- **One entry, one spread; never across a page turn** (Norman: "no individual pieces of content [...] are divided across multiple spreads"; McCoy; Golem; the Deathtrap; the Gauntlet). Norman adds the spread as a **cap on complexity**. [checked]
- **McCoy's list** (Q1) [checked]: work in spreads; "Don't split paragraphs between columns"; "Don't make me look for the monster's stat line"; page numbers on every reference; the reduction ladder text → bullets → table → diagram → illustration.
- **Notestine's lists** [checked]: parallel items; 3 to 5 items, "7 is (usually) the limit"; at most two levels of sub-bullets; emphasis only at the start of an item; indents of one em; bullets 30-50% of a capital's height.
- **Notestine's signals** [checked]: the same word in contents, heading and index; no decoration that looks like a code; headings given "room to breathe".
- **Paizo's order by when it is read** [checked]: what matters off-turn in the middle, what matters on the monster's turn at the bottom.
- **Butterick's floor** [read]: body text first; 45-90 characters; one of bold or italic, little of either; caps only for labels under a line, letterspaced.
- **The BDA's** [read]: headings "at least 20% larger"; "extra space around headings and between paragraphs"; left-aligned; white space "to group related content".
- **"The name is the loudest thing" and "labels left, values right"** were not found as stated rules; the nearest is Notestine's hierarchy ("meeting (or drawing) the audience's eyes to one place and then guiding them from there") and the Steel Compendium's label-then-value rows.

---

### Takeaways

1. **The sources reject "usable or beautiful" as a choice and settle it by place: the consulted parts are ordered and repeated; the mood goes in the frame, the art and the type.** Mörk Borg's rules summary has a grid; OSE separates its look from its principles; McCoy: "make it useful or beautiful."
   - *For 2d12:* a note only: the Action card is the most-consulted object in the book, the place where these designers allow the least experiment.
2. **"A rulebook is a teaching text, then a reference text" recurs in four sources**, with two answers: repeat what is needed instead of cross-referencing (the Gauntlet), or two documents, one to learn and one to look up (board games).
   - *For 2d12:* a note only: the book already has six documents; which ones teach and which are looked up is the question these sources ask.
3. **Consistency of anatomy is the findability rule cited most** ("repetition, predictable rhythms, and patterns create stability, trust, and confidence"; "Be consistent in your layout and design"), with grouping by a common region and white space, not by boxes inside boxes.
   - *For 2d12:* a note only: matches Lucas's "boxes inside boxes" dislike; the sources' tools for grouping are space and a rule, then a region.
4. **"Serious" in these designers' words is classic text type, few faces, no fake parchment or genre fonts, color kept for meaning.** Reece's "mid-century formalism"; Walmsley's classic faces; Bringhurst's "small palette of typefaces"; Tufte's greyscale text.
   - *For 2d12:* a note only: the current block's look (pills, a saturated bar, a color per trait word) is the set of things these sources list as ornament.
5. **Emphasis: one of bold or italic, as little as possible; bold for a lead-in at the start of a line, not in mid-line; caps only for short labels, letterspaced; real small caps or none.** Italic is the one style the dyslexia sources warn against; bold is the one they recommend.
   - *For 2d12:* a note only: trait words and Tier labels are labels (short, at the start), the case where caps or small caps and bold are accepted.
6. **Capitalized game terms split readers: "ungainly [...] but incredibly clear" against "makes sentences weirder" and "I'd prefer bold".** D&D 2024 chose capitals for defined terms and lowercase for the commonest words (action, turn, saving throw).
   - *For 2d12:* a note only: the book already capitalizes Bane and Boon; the debate is how far the rule spreads.
7. **Color for meaning is doubted by Paizo's publisher and must never be alone (WCAG 1.4.1); red against green is the pair that fails for about 1 man in 12.** 4e's green / red / black by frequency is the precedent, and it printed the word as well [lead for the word].
   - *For 2d12:* a note only: a frequency color stays readable if its word is printed next to it and the colors differ in lightness (the 3:1 note).
8. **Icons: few, large, distinct in shape, and with a plain-text form; past a handful they become a lookup.** Paizo kept three or four action glyphs and a text fallback; AD&D 2e's thirteen school icons are the counter-example.
   - *For 2d12:* a note only: a glyph that needs the legend is slower than the word it replaces.
9. **Measured facts that survive:** no serif-against-sans difference at reading sizes; dyslexia fonts did not help; italics slowed readers with dyslexia; dark-on-light reads better for normal vision, more so at small sizes; 45-90 characters a line; 120-145% leading; left-aligned text on the web (browser justification is "rudimentary").
   - *For 2d12:* a note only: the book is HTML read on screens first; a dark theme, small type and justified text are the three choices these facts weigh against; a switch to the other theme is what NN/g recommends keeping.
10. **Book feel in HTML is done with a serif text face, a measured column, sidenotes, small caps at section openings and greyscale text (Tufte CSS, Butterick).** The RPG reference sites read here are documentation templates and do not try.
   - *For 2d12:* a note only: the models for a book-feel HTML rulebook come from outside RPGs.
