# The look of an ability block in a printed rulebook (book feel, serious tone)

## 2026-11-16: for the book's Action cards ("much more book feel", "a more serious tone")

**The question (Lucas):** the book's Action cards are drawn like a web app (a rounded box, a saturated title bar in the frequency's color, sans-serif text, trait words each in a bright color, colored numbers, pill badges). How do published tabletop RPG books draw their ability, power, spell and stat blocks so that they feel like part of a book and read as serious, and what do designers, reviewers and players say works or fails? `card-layout.md` already covers WHERE the facts sit in a block; this file is about HOW the block is drawn.

**How far to trust this file.** One pass, by an agent (about 60 steps); not re-checked by a second. No raw report: everything found is here.
- [read] = seen in full: two book pages opened as images and looked at (Old-School Essentials' cleric spread, from Geek Native; a *13 True Ways* page, rendered from Pelgrane's free preview PDF, with its embedded font list); the Homebrewery's 5e stylesheet (a fan replica of the stat block, not Wizards' file); D&D Beyond's 2024 stat block article (text); Wikipedia's FF Scala Sans.
- [checked] = an opinion read on its page, with its date: EN World, Paizo's forum (including Erik Mona's reply), The Alexandrian and its comments, Geekerati, Gavin Norman's blog, Familiar Waves, Geek Native, Golem Productions, Failure Tolerated (Sean McCoy), Stargazer's World.
- [quoted] = seen as someone's quotation: Robert J. Schwalb's 2011 post (quoted by The Alexandrian and Geekerati).
- [lead] = memory or a search summary only: 4e's bar colors (green, red, black), Draw Steel's block, Pathfinder 2e's ruled header and trait boxes, 13th Age's frequency words, the FFG forum quotes, The One Ring 2e's "ancient tome" layout. **No 4e, Pathfinder 2e or Draw Steel page was seen as an image this pass.**
- Typeface names for 5e and Pathfinder come from readers who inspected PDFs, not from the publishers. Reddit, Giant in the Playground (Cloudflare) and RPG.net refuse readers and were left alone. `card-layout.md` holds where the facts sit in a block; not repeated here.

## Part 1. How each game draws its block

### D&D 4e (2008): the colored power bar
- **The look** [lead: memory, and a search summary of a 2008 forum repost of Wizards' "how to read a power" article; Giant in the Playground refused the reader]: each power sits under a full-width header bar in its frequency's color: **green for at-will, red for encounter, black (dark grey) for daily**; white text in the bar; the body is labeled lines (keyword line, action and range, Target, Attack, Hit, Miss, Effect). The same blocks were later sold as **power cards**.
- **"Looks like Magic cards / a video game hint book."** [checked, Geekerati, May 2024] "it becomes very much like reading spreadsheet outputs and less like reading abilities [...] almost as if they intended you to fill out cards [...] It looks far more like a Magic the Gathering card breakdown manual, or a video game hint book [...] than it does a role playing game manual." It holds that the "just World of Warcraft in table top form" sentiment "is due entirely to the layout and not the mechanics".
- **One of 4e's own designers said so.** [quoted twice, by The Alexandrian (2011) and Geekerati; Robert J. Schwalb's own post not opened] Schwalb (February 2011): "Fourth edition's presentation abandoned nearly everything familiar about the game's look. Eight years of 3rd edition, I think, created strong expectations about how the game should read and since the game didn't match the visual expectations, it certainly must not match the play experience." He reformatted powers to look like 3rd edition spells and asked whether the changes "might have been more palpable had we shifted back toward the old presentation, even if doing so meant that the game would be harder to learn."
- **Against: the format was the good part.** [checked, The Alexandrian, Feb 2011] Justin Alexander: "I've seen some people comparing the new powers format to Magic: The Gathering cards [...] But when you dig down into the real complaints [...] they tend to be either dissociated mechanics, abandoning the traditional D&D gameplay [...] or [...] miniatures"; "count me down pretty firmly in the camp of 'I like the format, I don't like the rules'". He reset 3rd edition spells in 4e styling to show it.
- **What readers of that post disliked in the look** [checked, comments on the same page, 2011]: "Still can't stand the light-on-dark headers, nor the dark fadeout backgrounds. Colour coding things not great either (especially red/green)." Another: the powers "in their color carded and damage formula glory, while very efficient, also has the unfortunate effect of emphasizing how boringly similar all those powers end up being"; "the occasional compact spell illustration (like in the 3rd edition spell section) might put a little more wonder back".
- [checked, EN World, "Presentation vs design... vs philosophy", 2020] "4E martial powers were formatted pretty much like 4E arcane or divine spells, with keywords, action required, attack vs defence, targets [...] 5E battlemaster maneuvers are pretty much [...] add superiority die [...] and I don't hear many complaints that they feel the same, or that they are like spells." (Presentation as a reason martial powers "felt like spells".)

### D&D 5e (2014): the parchment stat block
- **Typefaces** [checked, EN World thread "The Fonts of D&D 5th Edition", Jan 2016, quoting a closed Wizards forum; fan identification, not Wizards documentation]: **Bookmania** (body), **Mrs Eaves Small Caps** (chapter and section headings), **Mrs Eaves All Small Caps** (page footers), **Mrs Eaves XL Serif** (quoted in-world text), **Scala Sans** (sidebars and table body; **Scala Sans Small Caps** for their headings), Interstate (character sheet footers). Wikipedia's FF Scala Sans page [read] repeats "Sidebar/Table Body" and character sheets. So the book's prose is a warm serif (Bookman-like); the monster stat block and sidebars are set in a humanist sans.
- **The stat block's drawing** [read, as a fan replica: the Homebrewery's 5e theme stylesheet, which copies the book]: a light orange parchment fill (`#F2E5B5`) with a paper texture; a decorative top and bottom border (an image, the orange-gold bars); a soft drop shadow; the monster's name large in the heading serif, its size and type line in italic; **tapered red rules** (a long thin triangle, maroon `#9C2B1B`) separating the sections; the lines between the rules (AC, HP, Speed, the ability table) in **dark maroon** text (`#58180D`), the rest in black; section heads ("Actions") in **Scala Sans small caps** with a thin dark maroon underline; ability names in **bold italic** run into the paragraph ("***Multiattack.*** The dragon makes..."). Spells in the Player's Handbook are not boxed: name as a heading, an italic level-and-school line, four bold-labeled lines (Casting Time, Range, Components, Duration), then prose [read, SRD 5.1 text; from memory for the italic line].
- The color budget: one accent (maroon/red) for rules, the stat lines and headings; the box fill is the only other color.
- **2024** [read, D&D Beyond, Jan 2025, text only]: the change described is in content and order ("the section for ability scores and ability modifiers is no longer a list [...] It's now a table that lists the ability score, ability modifier, and corresponding saving throw bonus"), "designed to make them easier to run" "in the heat of battle". No source on the 2024 look's reception was found beyond reviews calling the Player's Handbook "better formatted overall" [lead, search summary].

### Pathfinder 2e (2019) and its ancestors
- **Typefaces** [checked, Paizo forum, Sep 2019, a reader's identification from the PDF]: headers **Taroca**; "Credits | Index | Sidebar | Tables | Items | Feats | Spells" **Good OT** (a condensed sans); story text **Sabon** italic; general text **Sabon**; sub-headers **Gin**. So, as in 5e, prose is a classic serif and the ability blocks (feats, spells, items) are a sans.
- **Starfinder (2017), for contrast** [checked, Paizo forum, Jul 2018, a reader's identification]: chapter titles **Axion**, section titles **GoodOT Condensed**, main text **GoodOT**, with Eurostile and others: a sans-only, science-fiction look.
- **Playtest feedback on the look** [checked, Paizo forum, Aug 2018, "The Book: Layout, Graphic Design & Tone / Language"]: "Action icons work really well in practice"; "The excess of traits & keywords is a huge problem. It's a manual that feels like it prioritises machine-readability so much that it actively obstructs human-readability"; "the tone and language is WAY TOO MUCH technical [...] it almost makes the game feel like a boardgame"; "Not only as a partially sighted/vision impaired [...] I find all icons/symbols irritating and cheap, in RPGs, just axe them"; a fix offered: "Use '1' [...] '2' [...] a Letter 'R'". Same thread: "Erik Mona specifically said that they're changing rarity indicator from color to something else."
- [checked, "Initial impressions from a layout and design perspective", 2018] "The placement of Traits in a left-justified bubble that indents the text that it interrupts. Yuck. [...] The is an eye-sore"; "I'm not sold on the symbols for actions. It seems like a solution in search of a problem. I don't see how this is an improvement over 'Action: Do this'"; the action symbols "interrupt the justification of the feat names [...] it should be FEAT NAME and then SYMBOL".
- **Paizo's own doubt about color** [checked, Erik Mona, publisher, Paizo forum, 19 May 2018]: "We're doing some experiments with symbols and color in the playtest book [...] I think even in-house we are skeptical that color is a useful way to delineate information, so that one is really on the fence [...] The symbols are a different story. We think they'll really help [...] Accessibility is a VERY important issue to us".
- **Accessibility of glyphs** [checked, "PF2E and Accessibility", May 2018]: a blind player, "if you continue with this representative symbol nonsense, the stat block will be absolutely useless to me"; "Starfinder icons for instance show up as a number. Unfortunately, that number is never the same"; copy-paste loses the glyph; a partially colorblind player: "over-reliance on color (particularly red/green for me) makes icons a lot less useful"; a poster's rule: "colour should be an enhancement on a presentation that's already clear [...] a clearly different shape". Another: "I do not like them in 4th Ed or SF (especially in SF, they look cheap, cheesy and gross)".

### Old-School Essentials (Necrotic Gnome, Gavin Norman)
- **What the praise is about: structure first.** [checked, Gavin Norman, Aug 2019] "When I refer to 'the Old-School Essentials style', I'm not referring to the visual design of the books (e.g. the choice of fonts, table colours, etc.) [...] each piece of content should fit onto a single page or spread [...] the reader will never have to flip a page back and forth"; the one-spread limit also "serves as a limitation on class complexity".
- **The look** [read: the cleric spread from the Classic Fantasy books, an image on Geek Native, 2020]: white page, two text columns, a book serif for the body with **bold run-in labels** ("Holy symbol:", "Turned undead:"); the class name in a tall condensed display face; **one accent color, a pale green tint**, used only as a flat band behind section headings and behind the class's summary box (Requirements, Prime requisite, Hit Dice...) and on the page-number tabs; table titles are a black bar with white condensed text; tables are ruled only under the header row; no rounded corners, no boxes inside boxes, black-and-white line art. Spells are not boxed at all (name heading, a few labeled lines, a paragraph) [lead: memory of the SRD].
- [checked, Familiar Waves, c. 2023] "Old School Essentials is obviously the standard bearer in communicating ideas effectively across a two page spread [...] 'control panel' two page spreads. While some say it lacks the charm of the original". [checked, Geek Native, Feb 2020] "I vastly prefer an RPG book with a layout meant to facilitate use at the table rather than written like a book with blocks of paragraphs."

### Shadowdark, Dolmenwood, Draw Steel, Daggerheart: one EN World thread (March to November 2026)
[checked, EN World "OSR Layout and Design Thread", 2026]
- **Shadowdark** (The Arcane Library): "perhaps the most viscerally functional OSR book ever published. If you want to see spreads done well, this is where to look"; "easy on the eyes, and I mean physiological, literal easy on my eyes"; "a lot of people seem to mistake aping the SD trade dress for actually doing the things it does well". Its body face is **Montserrat**, a geometric sans: "I don't love Montserrat as a body font, but that takes nothing away from how functional and readable it is"; "I don't like the rounded somewhat modern feel of it for fantasy applications." "SD's design really seems to be all about whitespace. Big chunks between paragraphs, large line spacing, paragraphs that are a couple of sentences [...] digestible despite the black and white colors".
- **Dolmenwood** (Necrotic Gnome): "the incredibly precise editing to get gorgeously justified paragraphs with those multi-tone colors to help your eye skip down from Header -> Section -> Subtopic and then well chosen emphasis bolding there. It is, I think, the most readable text I've seen"; the thread's opener added it "precisely because of how well it subtly uses colour to direct the readers eye and add structure to the dense layout."
- **Draw Steel** (MCDM, 2025): "my eyes are calling out for mercy. Black and White small scale text, no justification, no within-paragraph bolding to help digest"; "the font they chose isn't nearly as legible white on black as I would have wanted. Too many fine strokes in the font and it looks all wobbly." (Draw Steel's ability blocks themselves: [lead, memory] a dark header band with the name and its Heroic Resource cost, a keyword / type / distance / target grid, and three tier lines marked by small glyphs for ≤11, 12-16 and 17+.) [checked, Gaming Trend, 2025] the book has "a color coded tab on the pages to let you know which chapter you're on", one color for the whole classes section.
- **Daggerheart** (Darrington Press): "Lots of shapes and colors to break things up, and coded by section [...] callout boxes where it's a shaded color with fine darker lines top and bottom"; and "it's still dense AF though".
- The thread's models for learning layout: Into the Odd Remastered and Mörk Borg (both Johan Nohr), Shadowdark. One poster's floor: "14/12 point minimum [...] legible body font (alegreya in my case) and use enough white space".
- Related [checked, Golem Productions, 2025]: Pirate Borg's body text is **Alegreya** over "old paper textures [...] to evoke the same gritty feel"; "The art style must fit the game's atmosphere, and every design choice should reinforce the intended experience"; "There is one layout rule that stands above all else: It must be easy to use at the table!"

### 13th Age (Pelgrane Press): a book page, not a card
- **The look** [read: a page of the *13 True Ways* preview PDF, rendered and looked at; its embedded fonts listed]: no box around the monster. The name is a heading in **Mason Serif** (a display serif with flared, carved-looking capitals) in the book's one accent color (green); body text is **Warnock Pro**, justified, in two columns; an italic flavor sentence opens the block; the level and role line uses **small caps in brackets** ("2nd level troop [DEVIL]"); the attack is one bold run-in line ("**Fleshy limb +7 vs. AC**—6 damage"); each ability is an *italic run-in name* with a hanging indent ("*Stay whole:* At the start of its turn..."); only the defenses (AC, PD, MD, HP) sit on a pale green tint band at the foot. The page has a textured, ornamental top border and a gilt-framed painting. Class powers and spells carry their frequency as a word in the text ("at-will", "daily", "recharge") [lead: memory and a search summary of a 2013 review; not seen].
- A designer's note on the frequency *word* [lead, a search summary of Pelgrane's 2nd edition change list, PDF not opened]: "'Daily' spells and powers confused people, since we weren't using the words 'day' or 'daily' in any commonly understood meaning".

### Pathfinder 1e: dark bars on spell names, and printing
- [checked, Paizo forum, Nov 2010] in the printed *Advanced Player's Guide* spell names are "light writing on a dark brown box"; one reader's PDF viewer showed "black writing on a dark brown box which is almost unreadable" (a fault of the viewer, solved by turning off "Line Weights").
- **Printer-friendly versions** [checked, Paizo forum, 2013]: readers want "shutting off a) background textures, b) top, bottom, and edge dressing, and c) photos"; "Even if you print in black and white there is still the border on every page that uses a lot of ink"; a Paizo staff answer: "Our production process at the moment does not currently have time allotment for an additional PDF option beyond our Lite PDFs for hardcover releases." Chaosium's newer PDFs put backgrounds on a layer the reader can hide [lead, search summary of Chaosium's page].

### Warhammer Fantasy Roleplay 3rd edition (Fantasy Flight, 2009): real action cards
- The game printed its actions as physical cards with custom dice and tokens. [checked, comments on Stargazer's World, May 2010] "the extras (cards, chits, etc.) seemed more of a hindrance to play [...] I have to call 'the boardgame feel' [...] they are good at boardgames, both in mechanics and (most definitely) in presentation"; against: "it's neither a board game nor a pseudo-computer game. It is a classic ROLE playing game with some board game gimmicks"; "During play we do not use any rule book!" [lead, search summaries of FFG forum archives] "not a RPG but a sort of basterdised board game"; "having all that information physically in front of you makes the game more visceral, not less".

### Mothership (Tuesday Knight Games, Sean McCoy): the designer on fantasy's "UI"
- [checked, Sean McCoy, Failure Tolerated, c. 2019] "In fantasy the most common UI theme is illuminated manuscripts and tomes. Everyone wants things to look like old books, because that's all we've got. In sci-fi you have every piece of real or imagined software to pull from." "If people have a complaint about Mothership in the layout department it's that the text is too small or that there's white text on black [...] The biggest rule is: make it useful or beautiful." His order: "Can this text be bullet points? Can these bullet points be a table? Can this table be a diagram?"; "Don't make me flip the page unless I'm completely moving on from this section."
- Mörk Borg, by contrast [checked, Familiar Waves]: "extensively criticized for its almost-confrontational layout, with many saying that it is style over substance"; the reviewer defends it: "the layout and art in the core book conveys its tone non-verbally".

## Part 2. Across games

**Two type families, split by job.** In every book whose fonts were found, the prose and the reference blocks are set differently: 5e prose in Bookmania (a Bookman serif), its stat blocks and sidebars in Scala Sans; Pathfinder 2e prose in Sabon (a Garamond-style serif), its feats, spells and items in Good OT (a condensed sans); 13th Age is the exception, all serif (Warnock Pro) with a carved display serif (Mason) for names. Display faces with an "old" flavor are kept to names and headings (Mrs Eaves small caps, Taroca, Mason). Starfinder and Shadowdark use a sans for the body (GoodOT; Montserrat); Shadowdark's was called both "functional and readable" and of a "rounded somewhat modern feel [...] for fantasy".

**What is a box and what is a line.** 5e: one tinted box (the stat block) with rules inside, no box around a spell. Pathfinder 2e: a ruled line under the name, traits in small boxes [lead: memory of the books, not seen this pass]. OSE and 13th Age: no box at all; a tint band behind a heading or the defenses. 4e: a full colored header bar over every power, light text on dark. The forum complaints land on the 4e end ("light-on-dark headers", "dark fadeout backgrounds"; Pathfinder 1e's dark spell-name boxes; Mothership's white on black; Draw Steel's fine type "white on black").

**Run-in names.** 5e (***bold italic***), 13th Age (*italic*), OSE (**bold**) all start an ability's paragraph with its name in the same line, the oldest book convention for a definition list.

**Color: how much and for what.** The books praised for reading well use one accent color, structurally: OSE's pale green bands, 13th Age's green names and tint band, 5e's maroon rules and stat lines, Dolmenwood's "multi-tone colors to help your eye skip down from Header -> Section -> Subtopic". Color as a *code* (4e's frequency bars, the Pathfinder playtest's rarity colors) drew the doubts: Paizo's publisher was "skeptical that color is a useful way to delineate information", and rarity by color alone was changed for the final book (to a labeled trait). Red against green is named twice as a problem (4e, Pathfinder).

**Frequency and cost marks.** Four ways were found: a colored bar (4e); a word in the block (13th Age, OSE, 5e's spell level line); a glyph next to the name (Pathfinder 2e's action icons, praised as fast by some and "irritating and cheap", "a solution in search of a problem" by others; the glyph is lost in copy-paste and screen readers unless backed by text); a number-and-letter alternative proposed by readers ("1", "2", "R").

### Takeaways

1. **The "gamey" verdict attaches to the look more than to the rules, by one of the designers' own account.** Schwalb (4e) thought the format told readers the game "must not match the play experience"; Geekerati: the World of Warcraft and Magic comparisons were "due entirely to the layout". Justin Alexander disagreed: the format was 4e's best part.
   - *For 2d12:* a note only: the two views agree that a block's look tells readers what kind of game it is before they read a rule.
2. **The look most called "like cards" is a full-width colored header bar over every block, in a color that codes the frequency.** That is 4e's, and the comments name its parts: "light-on-dark headers", "color carded", "Colour coding [...] (especially red/green)". WFRP 3rd edition's physical cards drew "the boardgame feel".
   - *For 2d12:* a note only: this is the drawing the book's Action cards use today (a saturated bar by frequency).
3. **The bookish blocks found share four habits:** no rounded box (either no box at all, or one flat tinted box for a whole creature); thin rules or a tint band for structure instead of filled bars; the name and run-in labels in bold or italic of the body face; small caps for tags and section heads.
   - *For 2d12:* a note only: 5e, 13th Age and OSE show all four.
4. **One accent color, used for structure, is what the praised books share.** OSE (pale green bands), 13th Age (green), 5e (maroon), Dolmenwood ("subtly uses colour to direct the readers eye"). No praised book found colors each keyword or number.
   - *For 2d12:* a note only: today's trait words each in their own color have no counterpart in the books read.
5. **Color as the only carrier of a meaning is the most criticised use**, by players with color blindness and by Paizo's publisher; the Pathfinder playtest's rarity colors were replaced. The readers' rule: color "should be an enhancement on a presentation that's already clear".
   - *For 2d12:* a note only: a frequency shown by a word as well as a color passes that test; a color alone does not.
6. **Glyphs split readers.** Pathfinder 2e kept its action icons; the arguments against were screen readers, copy-paste, low light and "cheap"; for, speed once learned. 13th Age moved away from the word "daily" because it misled.
   - *For 2d12:* a note only: the frequency label's wording and its mark are two decisions.
7. **Serious fantasy books pair a classic book serif for reading with a restrained display face for names**: Bookmania with Mrs Eaves (5e), Sabon with Taroca (Pathfinder 2e), Warnock Pro with Mason (13th Age). The blocks themselves are often a humanist or condensed sans (Scala Sans, Good OT), so a sans in a block is not by itself what reads as an app.
   - *For 2d12:* a note only: the book's current Action card face is a system UI stack (`system-ui`, Segoe UI, Roboto), which no book found uses.
8. **Reference speed in the praised books comes from structure and space, not decoration**: one topic on one spread (OSE, Shadowdark), whitespace and short paragraphs (Shadowdark), bold labels and justified, carefully broken text (Dolmenwood). Draw Steel's dense unjustified pages are the counter-example readers named.
   - *For 2d12:* a note only: the praise is for the page as a whole; a block is read inside it.
9. **Dark backgrounds and fine type are the most repeated legibility complaint** (Mothership's white on black, Draw Steel "white on black", Pathfinder 1e dark boxes, 4e headers), and printers ask for backgrounds and edge dressing they can switch off.
   - *For 2d12:* a note only: a book page may be printed; a print stylesheet without fills is the publishers' missing option readers asked for.
10. **"Like an old book" is the default fantasy idiom, and a designer calls it the only one fantasy has** (McCoy). Mörk Borg shows the opposite pole: a loud look accepted because it carries the tone, and criticised as "style over substance".
    - *For 2d12:* a note only: a serious tone is a choice among idioms, not the absence of one.
