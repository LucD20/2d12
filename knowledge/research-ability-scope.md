# Ability scope: how much one Action should do, and the kinds of Actions besides attacks

## 2026-11-14: "are our Actions doing too much?" (Lucas)

**The question (Lucas):** Actions may do too much; an Action can also be a simple action compression (like many feats in other systems), or creative, interacting with rules that don't involve numbers. We don't want the Paladin's turn to be "Advance, Smite, attack" every time.

### Takeaways

1. **Complexity is a budget, and it belongs where it is seen least often.** Magic: The Gathering's New World Order (2008) fought "complexity creep" by keeping the cards you meet most (commons) simple (a common shouldn't affect more than one other card on the battlefield) and moving complexity up to rarer cards, not removing it. Rosewater's "lenticular design" hides depth inside simple-looking cards, so beginners read them plainly and experts find more.
   - *For 2d12:* the Actions used every round (the Hallmarks, the Reactions) should be the simplest; Encounter Picks can carry a little more, the Apex the most. Today it is the opposite on the Paladin: Smite, the every-round card, has the most text (133 words over three Versions).
2. **Feats that change what you can do age better than feats that raise numbers.** Pathfinder 2e posters credit its feats with "going wide": more things you can do, few stacking bonuses, so almost every feat is viable; Paizo avoids pure numeric feats because they move the power level unpredictably.
   - *For 2d12:* the Paladin's Passives are mostly "+X damage" (Radiant Zeal, Searing Blows, Reckoning): the kind that is invisible and doesn't change a decision.
3. **Action compression is its own kind of option.** Pathfinder 2e players name "action economy compressors" as a category: Quick Jump (a Long Jump for 1 action instead of 2), Sudden Charge (two Strides and a Strike for 2 actions). They change the shape of a turn without adding a new effect, and they are easy to read.
   - *For 2d12:* "Advance, then Shove", "Stand and Advance", "Advance and Interact" (now allowed by the decided Advance rule), "Lay On Hands as you pass" are cards with no new numbers that still change the turn.
4. **Non-damage abilities are where characters differ.** 4e players say utility powers (which "should not deal damage") became the main way characters were told apart, especially once skill powers arrived; the attack powers were criticised as samey. D&D 2024 was criticised for cutting "ribbons" (features mostly for flavor, small in power) that "help construct the class".
   - *For 2d12:* the Paladin has almost none: every card is damage, healing or a defense number. A ribbon or a rule-bender (what your holy light does to Hidden creatures, carrying a Down ally, sensing the Profane) gives the class identity at no budget cost.
5. **Rote turns come from one best default.** Turn-based game writers: when the attack command is always right, players stop thinking; the fix offered most is a few simple everyday options with different effects (a delaying hit, a positioning hit), each usable every turn, so the choice depends on the board.
   - *For 2d12:* the 1 / 2 / 3 AP Versions make each turn a budget problem ("biggest Smite I can afford, then attacks"), not a board problem. Two or three small, different Round options beat one card in three sizes.
6. **A separate small economy for small things.** Draw Steel splits main actions, maneuvers (small, often free) and triggered actions (one a round, with standout signature ones such as the Censor's My Life for Yours).
   - *For 2d12:* 0 AP and 0R options (Mark Quarry, Beacon's Step) already play this role; the Paladin could have one 0 AP everyday trick instead of a bigger Smite.
