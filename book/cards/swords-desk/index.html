Pressed. Pressed always has a value, such as Pressed 1. Your [Strike] Actions take that many Pressure Banes (at most 2 count). If you would be Pressed twice, only the highest value applies.
  • The name. Pressed says which Bane it is, so "Pressed 2" explains itself and the cap of 2 comes with it. Others: Reeling (the rule's own example: "a blow that leaves you reeling") · Hampered · Off-balance · Checked · Rattled (reads as fear) · Fettered (reads as Immobilized) · Harried (close to "Harry", an Archer idea you kept for later).
  • Strikes only. Saves and skill rolls are untouched, which keeps it clear of Frightened.
  • A value, like Vulnerable: the same sentence shape and the same stacking rule.
  • Who else can use it: the Bestiary's "1 Pressure Bane on its next roll" could become "Pressed 1 (EoT)" later; a net, a whip, a hail of arrows.

Analysis

  • The decision it gives. Whom to bind: the foe that hits hardest, not the one nearest to dying. And then to stay: the bind lasts only while you threaten the target, so walking away to another fight lets its blade go. The foe has a way out too: step out of your Range, and pay the movement.
  • Its pair. Seizing Cut: one hand free, move the body. Thwart Cut: both hands on the sword, hold the blade. Position against protection.
  • The Paladin with it. Judge takes his Power Modifier off the first damage a Judged foe deals to an ally each round. Shield of Faith takes damage off one hit, as a Reaction. Thwart Cut works one step earlier and on another number: the foe's roll, against anyone. It gives a two-handed Paladin a protector's play that costs no Reaction.
  • Against the classes' cards. Taunted is 1 Bane on a foe's Actions against anyone but you. Bound is 1 Bane on its Strikes against everyone, and needs your sword in reach.
  • The rules it touches. Named Boons and Banes (Chapter 1). EoT is the end of the affected creature's turn. Threatening (Chapter 3). "Bound" is a defined word on the card, as "Judged" is on Judge.
  • The name. Thwart Cut is the Zwerchhau in English. With your liking for the rarer names, the card could carry the German: Zwerchhau.

Analysis

  • The decision it gives. Where the foe goes. One square, any direction: off an ally it was Engaged with (a friend's bow is no longer Doomed), into a second ally's reach for a flank, out of a doorway, out of its cover, or into Hazardous Terrain. And whether to use it at all: its Tier 2 hits with the die alone, so a Weapon Attack hits harder when the foe is already where you want it.
  • The Tier 3. Grabbed: the foe is Immobilized and only you can move it, until it pays 1 AP to Break Free, you let go, or you leave its reach. Your hand is then busy, so you can't use Seizing Cut again while you hold. Letting go is the next decision. The simpler Tier 3 is "Slid 2".
  • Its pair, ahead of time. The one-handed sword moves the foe's body. The Longsword, in two hands, is left the foe's blade: the bind.
  • The Paladin with it. He has no everyday way to move one enemy; his pushes are once a fight and hit a crowd. With Hold the Line and Judge, placing one foe a round is what a protector wants. At Weapon +1 he sees the Slid on 65% of uses.
  • Against the classes' cards. Binding Light Immobilizes for a round on a Tier 2 or 3, for 2 AP. This Grabbed is a quarter as likely, ends for 1 AP, and costs you your hand. Trick Shots' Knock Back pushes for [Mod] damage at range; this slides 1 in reach, with an empty hand.
  • The rules it touches. Slid (Chapter 3): you choose the path; no Reactions; Steadiness resists it, so the card writes no size limit. Grabbed (Chapter 8). "A free hand" is the book's phrase.
  • The history. Lecküchner's messer text, which was read: "you should fence with one hand with the Messer and you should have the other one on the back. If you want to fight with the empty hand..." Research: what the fencing texts say

  • Meyer (1570) names two foundations. The long sword, "as a foundation of all fencing". The dusack, "a beginning and foundation for all weapons that are used in one hand". The dusack is a wooden trainer; its steel relatives are the messer and the sabre.
  • Fiore (about 1400) teaches the sword in one hand before the sword in two, "to make my system easier to learn". Another copy of his book gives the reverse order.
  • The big two-hander is the specialist's weapon in every source read. Di Grassi (1594): "as a galleon among many galleys". Figueyredo (1651): the montante has "sixteen simple rules, and sixteen composed", each a drill for a situation: hold a street, a narrow street, "Guarding a Lady", against shieldsmen.
  • The rapier is "a newly discovered practice for the Germans" for Meyer, and he teaches it last. Silver (1599) calls it "the worst weapon", for its length.
  • A Chinese saying: "a hundred days for the dao, a thousand for the spear, ten thousand for the jian".
  • The names are traps. The historical longsword has a two-handed grip; what games call a longsword is an arming sword. "Bastard sword" is the period word for the sword of one or two hands. "Claymore" is two weapons. "Flamberge" is wrong for the wave-bladed two-hander.
