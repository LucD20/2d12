# Swords: which were taught first, which were a specialist's, the families, the names, and what each was known for

## 2026-11-16: for the weapons pass (the Swords group: six weapons, two lines of Basic, Advanced, Exotic)

**The three questions.** (1) Which swords did historical fencing sources and HEMA practitioners treat as the easiest, the first taught or the foundation, and which as the hardest or most specialised? (2) Which historically grounded families and progressions of swords exist (hands, when and where, size, what the name means), and where are the naming traps? (3) What was each sword known to be good at, in the sources' words?

**How far to trust this file.** One pass, by an agent (about 35 steps); not re-checked by a second. There is no raw report: everything found is here. History is reported, nothing is proposed as a design.
- [read] = a treatise's text read on its page, in the English translation or transcription Wiktenauer shows: Joachim Meyer 1570 (the sword, dusack and rapier introductions), Fiore de'i Liberi (the prologues), George Silver 1599 (*Paradoxes of Defence*), Giacomo di Grassi (the 1594 English translation, the two hand sword), Diogo Gomes de Figueyredo 1651 (the simple rules of the montante), Sigmund Ringeck's gloss of Liechtenauer (parts), Johannes Lecküchner (parts). Also the D&D SRD 5.2 weapon table.
- [quoted] = seen as someone's quotation: Ewart Oakeshott, a Royal Armouries curator, Paulus Jovius and two arms historians, all inside John Clements's two ARMA essays.
- [checked] = a modern statement read on its page: Wikipedia articles (a tertiary source: good for names and dates, weak for weights), myArmoury's glossary (reproduced from a Simon and Schuster reference book), ARMA essays, one tai chi school's blog.
- [lead] = a search summary or memory only, to open before it is quoted to anyone. Every line so marked below.
- Silver is quoted from both versions on his page (the modernised one and the 1599 spelling, with the long s written as s); the words are his, the spelling is mixed.
- **Weak spots, said plainly:** no museum catalogue was opened (sizes come from Wikipedia, myArmoury and the ARMA essays' quotations); nothing by Matt Easton on teaching order was found (one article of his on the 1890s army sword was opened and says nothing on it); Hutton, Roworth, Angelo, Godinho, Godfrey, I.33's text and all kenjutsu were not read; "the sabre is quick to teach" has no source here. Reddit and the HEMA forums were not opened.
- `weapons-bows-light.md` already covers how games give a weapon its trick: not repeated. Five lines on how one game tells swords apart are at the end of Q2.

## Q1. Easiest and first taught; hardest and most specialised

1. **Meyer (1570) names two foundations: the long sword for all fencing, the dusack for everything held in one hand.** [read] The book's first part is headed "Firstly, the Sword as a foundation of all fencing". The dusack part opens: "After the foundation was laid previously in the [section on] fencing with the sword, the dusack now follows, which takes its origin from the sword as the correct source of all fencing (both that directed with one hand and that with two). Therefore, I want to place this here—not only because it is the most commonly-used [weapon] for us Germans (following the sword) but also because it is a beginning and foundation for all weapons that are used in one hand". His order is sword, dusack, rapier, then dagger and staff weapons ("the half staff as a foundation of all long weapons").
   - *For 2d12:* a note only: one source gives a two-handed foundation and a one-handed foundation side by side, which is the shape of two Basic weapons.
2. **Meyer's dusack is a practice weapon as much as a sidearm.** [checked, Wikipedia] "Practice dussacks had a short, thick, single-edged blade measuring between 25–38 in (640–970 mm) long. A dussack was usually made of wood", with a hole cut for the hand; the name is "from Czech tesák 'cleaver; hunting sword', lit. 'fang'". The steel sidearm of that name was a sabre blade on a sword hilt ("Säbel auf Teutsch gefasst").
   - *For 2d12:* a note only: the historical beginner's cutting sword is a wooden trainer; its steel cousins are the messer before it and the sabre after it.
3. **Meyer calls the rapier new and foreign, a thing a German must learn because others use it.** [read] "Concerning rapier fencing, which is currently a very necessary and useful exercise, there is no doubt that so much of it is a newly discovered practice for the Germans (since it was brought to us by other peoples)." And in the sword part: "thrusting with the sword has been abolished for us Germans".
   - *For 2d12:* a note only: in the German sources the thrusting sword is the late, imported study, placed after the cutting ones.
4. **Fiore (about 1400-1410) starts with the body, not a sword; the sword in one hand comes before the sword in two.** [read] The Wiktenauer page lists the sections: "abrazare or grappling; daga [...]; spada a un mano, the use of the sword in one hand (also called 'the sword without the buckler'); spada a dui mani, the use of the sword in two hands; spada en arme, the use of the sword in armor [...]; azza, plays of the poleaxe in armor; lancia [...]; and mounted combat". One prologue: "I will begin with grappling"; and the reason for the order: "to make my system easier to learn, by using the same principles of the guards, the Master, the Remedy and the Counter throughout it, just as you see first in the section on Grappling." **Caution:** another copy's prologue on the same page gives the reverse order ("we will commence first on horse with lance and with sword and with wrestling [...] and finally wrestling on foot and the play of the dagger"), so the order is a teaching choice of one manuscript, not a law.
   - *For 2d12:* a note only: Fiore's one-handed sword is a short section of a few plays; his two-handed sword is the long one.
5. **Silver (1599) ranks weapons by advantage, not by ease, and puts the rapier last.** [read] "First I will begin with the worst weapon, an imperfect and insufficient weapon, and not worth the speaking of, but now being highly esteemed [...] That is, the single rapier, and rapier and poniard." Then, each line as he wrote it: "The single sword has the vantage against the single rapier." "The sword and buckler has advantage against the sword and target, the sword and dagger, or rapier and poniard." "The two handed sword has the vantage against the sword and target, the sword and buckler, the sword and dagger, or rapier and poniard." Above it only the weapons "of weight" (battle axe, halberd, bill) and the staves. The title page: "the short sword has advantage over the long sword or the long rapier."
   - *For 2d12:* a note only: Silver's ladder inside the swords is rapier, single sword, sword with a companion, two-handed sword; it is a ladder of who beats whom.
6. **Silver's complaint about the long rapier is its length, not its difficulty.** [read] "the cause of the great slaughter, and sundry hurts done by long rapiers, consists not in their long reach, dangerous thrusts, nor cunningness of the Italian fight, but in the inconvenient length, and unwieldiness". He also records how London's schools split the teaching: "at the single sword, sword and dagger, & sword and buckler, they forbid the thrust, & at the single rapier, and rapier & dagger, they forbid the blow."
   - *For 2d12:* a note only: in 1599 a school taught "sword" as the cutting art and "rapier" as the thrusting art, as two courses.
7. **The big two-handed sword is the specialist's weapon in every source read: a strong man's, with its own purpose (many opponents).** [read, di Grassi] "because one may with it (as a galleon among many galleys) resist many Swords, or other weapons: Therefore in the wars, it is used to be place near unto the Ensign or Ancient, for the defense thereof [...] And it is accustomed to be carried in the City, as well by night as by day, when it so chances that a few are constrained to withstand a great many. And because his weight and bigness, requires great strength, therefore those only are allotted to the handling thereof, which are mighty and big to behold, great and strong in body, of stout and valiant courage." [quoted, in ARMA's essay] "its use was considered as special skill, often meriting extra pay."
   - *For 2d12:* a note only: this is the clearest "hardest, opened last" statement found, and it comes with a built-in job.
8. **The montante has a written syllabus: sixteen simple rules, then sixteen composed ones** (Figueyredo, 1651: "Including sixteen simple rules, and sixteen composed") [read]. Each rule is a drill for a situation (takeaway 20). In Spain it was the master's own tool [checked, Wikipedia]: "used by the master of arms to signal the end of a duel or a spar between two alumns, leading to the idiom meter el montante"; later "used by masters of arms, by bodyguards and occasionally in self-defense situations".
   - *For 2d12:* a note only: a simple set and a composed set is a period example of a weapon learned in two steps.
9. **The Liechtenauer tradition teaches one sword three ways, and the name "long sword" is one of the ways.** [checked, Wikipedia] "The German langes schwert ('long sword') in 15th and 16th-century manuals does not denote a type of weapon, but the technique of fencing with both hands at the hilt, contrasting with kurzes schwert ('short sword') used of fencing with the same weapon, but with one hand gripping the blade". The armoured half-sword fight is the later, harder chapter in Ringeck's gloss as Wiktenauer lays it out [read: the order of the page].
   - *For 2d12:* a note only: in the German school the advanced material is a second grip on the same sword, not a second sword.
10. **A modern instructor's view (John Clements, ARMA): the single sword first, and skills carry upward by family.** [checked] "teaching the core principles and foundational concepts for the single sword as a foundation." "If you learn short sword or messer with buckler you apply that to sword and dagger. [...] If you learn longsword you apply it to poleax and great-sword. And fighting with the dagger applies to any short blade". And: "there is no 'best'" weapon. A search summary says most HEMA clubs start beginners on the longsword because clubs, loaner swords and sources are plentiful [lead].
    - *For 2d12:* a note only: "longsword leads to great sword", "messer leads to the paired one-handed fights" are a practitioner's words for which weapon opens which.
11. **The Chinese saying, in the wording found: 百日刀, 千日槍, 萬日劍,** "Hundred days of the sabre training, thousand days of the spear training, ten thousand days of the sword training" [checked, a tai chi school's blog, 2020], which adds: "the saying is not a guide to how long training actually is." Wikipedia's Dao article gives a softer version without the characters: "it takes a week to attain competence with a dao/saber, a month to attain competence with a qiang/spear, and a year to attain competence with a jian" [checked]. Titles there: the dao "The General of Weapons", the jian "the Gentleman of Weapons". Other variants (a month for the staff, a year for the dao, a lifetime for the spear) are memory only [lead]. No dated first source for any version was found.
    - *For 2d12:* a note only: the best-known "easy sword, hard sword" proverb opposes a single-edged chopper to a straight double-edged sword.
12. **Not verified: that the military sabre or broadsword was the quick weapon to teach and the smallsword or rapier the long schooling.** What was read: the singlestick "began as a way of training soldiers in the use of backswords (such as the sabre or the cutlass)" and "bears approximately the same relationship to the backsword as the foil to the small sword" [checked, Wikipedia]; a HEMA blog lists Angelo, Roworth, Hutton and Waite as the simple, well-supported sabre manuals for beginners [checked, HEMA Misfits, 2018]. A search summary says Angelo's ten lessons (1798) were the recruits' drill for generations and that Hope's 1707 *New, Short and Easy Method* joined broadsword and smallsword in one theory [lead]. No period sentence comparing learning times was found.
    - *For 2d12:* a note only: the sabre as "the easy one" is common talk but stands on no quotation in this file.

## Q2. Families, sizes, names

**Progressions that the sources themselves state** (no line is proposed; these are the lineages found):
- **Straight, double-edged, growing:** the one-handed knightly sword, then the long-gripped longsword / bastard sword, then the two-hander. [checked, Wikipedia] The arming sword became "a sidearm [...] contrasting with the two-handed, heavier longswords"; "Zweihänder swords developed from the longswords of the Late Middle Ages [...] the final stage in the trend of making very large swords, which started in the 14th century, and ended in the 16th"; the montante is "a middle step between a standard European longsword and a German zweihänder".
- **The German single-edged knife family:** Bauernwehr (a peasant's knife), langes Messer or grosses Messer (one hand), Kriegsmesser (two hands) [checked]; the dusack as its practice form and successor [read, Meyer; in one transcription of Lecküchner's messer text on Wiktenauer the word Messer is replaced by "Duseggen"].
- **The curved sabre family:** steppe sabres, then kilij, shamshir, talwar ("The curved 'scimitar' sword family includes the shamshir, kilij, talwar, pulwar, and nimcha"); the Hungarian szabla, the hussar's sabre, the western military sabre; its straight twin the backsword ("a backsword could be called a straight sabre, and vice versa"); the singlestick as its trainer [checked].
- **The thrusting line:** estoc (armour), rapier (the civilian thrust), smallsword, then the duelling épée [checked: the knightly sword "developed into [...] the side-sword, the rapier"; the smallsword "was the immediate predecessor of the French duelling sword"].
- **A soldier's pair:** the Landsknecht's katzbalger at the belt and two-hander on the shoulder [checked].
- **China's pair:** dao and jian (takeaway 11).

**The naming traps.**
13. **"Longsword".** The historical longsword has a two-handed grip. [checked, Wikipedia] The modern word "only resurfaced in the 2000s in the context of reconstruction of the German school"; before that "'long sword' [...] referred to the rapier"; and Silver's "long sword" [read] is a long one-handed sword set against his "short sword". D&D's longsword (1d8, Versatile 1d10, 3 lb. [read]) is an arming sword or small bastard sword by these terms.
    - *For 2d12:* a note only: the same word names three different weapons in three periods.
14. **"Bastard sword" is the period word; "hand-and-a-half" is modern.** [checked] "'bastard sword' is the oldest, its use being contemporaneous with the weapon's heyday"; "'hand-and-a-half sword' is relatively modern (from the late 19th century)"; in 1540 Henry VIII's Masters of Defence "listed two handed sword and bastard sword as two separate items"; Elizabethans said "'short', 'bastard', and 'long'" for blade length and "'two-handed' for any sword that could be wielded by two hands".
    - *For 2d12:* a note only: period English already had a three-step ladder of words, and "bastard" was the middle one.
15. **"Broadsword"** was never the medieval sword's name: [checked] "wrongly labelled a broadsword by antiquarians"; it properly names the basket-hilted swords of the 17th-18th centuries. **"Claymore"** is two weapons: the two-handed Highland sword and the basket-hilted one; the word is attested only in 1772-1773, for both, and which came first is disputed [checked]. **"Zweihänder"** is itself a later word beside the period Beidhänder / Bidenhänder [checked]; "greatsword" and "great sword" are also loose modern labels.
    - *For 2d12:* a note only: of these, only "claymore" needs a qualifier every time it is used.
16. **"Flamberge" is not the wave-bladed sword.** [checked, myArmoury] "In modern times 'flamberge' has been erroneously applied to swords having wavy blades, which were actually called flambards or flammards"; the German word is Flammenschwert. Flamberge was a romance hero's sword name, later a mocking French word for a showy rapier. And the blade [quoted, ARMA]: "visually striking but really no more effective in its cutting than a straight one."
    - *For 2d12:* a note only: the correct exotic name is Flammenschwert (or flammard); the history gives it looks, not a function.
17. **"Messer" names by the hilt, not the size** [checked]: "a large Bauernwehr might well have also been called a Messer, or a large Großes Messer might have been called a Kriegsmesser". **"Estoc" and "tuck" are one word.** **"Scimitar"** is a western umbrella word for the curved family, not one sword.
    - *For 2d12:* a note only: "grosses Messer" and "Kriegsmesser" overlap in period use; the two-hands line between them is a modern tidy-up.

**How one game tells swords apart** [read, D&D SRD 5.2]: Greatsword 2d6, Heavy, Two-Handed, **Graze**; Longsword 1d8, Versatile (1d10), **Sap**; Rapier 1d8, Finesse, **Vex**; Scimitar 1d6, Finesse, Light, **Nick**; Shortsword 1d6, Finesse, Light, **Vex**. The split is the die, the hands and one trick; the short sword and the scimitar sit with the light weapons. Pathfinder 2e's sword group and Draw Steel were not read for this file.

## Q3. What each sword was known to be good at

18. **The longsword: the bind, the winding, and five named cuts.** [read, Ringeck's gloss] The verse: "Learn five cuts / From the right hand". On the Zwerchhau: "the thwart-hew breaks all hews which are hewn from above down", done by springing aside and striking "with the short edge to his left side such that you catch their cut in your hilt and hit them in the head." On the bind: "in the binding of the swords you shall not omit the fleshwounds to the nearest opening". Meyer [read] reduces all cuts to four ("High Cut, Wrath Cut, Middle or Crossing Cut, and the Low Cut") and names Squinter and Crooked among the rest; the full list of five (Zornhau, Krumphau, Zwerchhau, Schielhau, Scheitelhau) is memory [lead]. "Feeling" [read, on Meyer's page]: "test whether the opponent fights hard or soft".
    - *For 2d12:* a note only: the longsword's fame is a cut that is also the parry, and the fight after the blades touch.
19. **The same sword against armour: half-sword and the murder-stroke.** [read, Ringeck] "When you set the point to his face with the halfswording and he displaces this, so strike him with the pommel to the other side of his head. Or spring with the right foot behind his left, and go with the pommel over his right shoulder in front and round the neck and therefore pull him over your right leg." [checked, Wikipedia] The Mordhau is "holding the sword inverted, with both hands gripping the blade, and hitting the opponent with the pommel or crossguard [...] essentially use the sword as a mace or hammer"; "a slice or a cleaving blow from a sword is virtually useless against iron or steel plate".
    - *For 2d12:* a note only: three things in one technique family: a thrust with a hand on the blade, a hammer blow, a throw.
20. **The montante: named rules for named situations** [read, Figueyredo]. "This rule serves to deter people in a street and impede them moving from one end to another"; "This rule serves to fight in a narrow street"; "This rule is called Guarding a Lady, presuming that she hides behind your shoulders, and you wish to defend her"; "Guarding a Cloak, because it is used to defend one which has fallen to the ground"; "Galley Gangway"; "for fighting with people in front and behind"; "This rule serves against shieldsmen." The motions are wide cuts chained with steps (talho from the right, revez from the left), a thrust, a pommel strike.
    - *For 2d12:* a note only: the source's own list is: hold a street, fight in a narrow place, guard a person, guard an object or spot, fight front and back, beat shields.
21. **The two-hander against many: big circular cuts, to frighten as much as to hit** [read, di Grassi]. Its users "(forasmuch as they are to encounter many, and to the end they may strike the more safely, and amaze them with the fury of the Sword) do altogether use to deliver great edge blows"; he treats "the defense of circular blows, of the two hand sword, when it endeavors to oppose itself against many" apart from the fight "one to one". It stands "near unto the Ensign [...] for the defense thereof". [checked, Wikipedia, on the Flammenschwert] used "for single-fights, protecting castle/town walls, or sometimes to protect the banner".
    - *For 2d12:* a note only: di Grassi separates two uses of one weapon: against a crowd, and one to one.
22. **Breaking pikes: attested, and doubted.** For [quoted, ARMA]: Paulus Jovius "described the two-hand great sword as being used by Swiss soldiers to chop the shafts of pikes at the battle of Fornovo in 1495"; Kamniker and Krenn: the double-pay men stood "in the front ranks for a long time to strike down the opposing pikes and to hack out breaches". Against [quoted, same authors]: "it would become unusable, as soon as the opposing forces collided [...] which created a thick melee", the adoption "was very limited", and "around the middle of the 16th century it [...] mutated into a form of guard and ceremonial weapon"; the Swiss "outlawed [it] as a frontline weapon". [checked, Wikipedia] "The veracity of this tradition is disputed, but it dates back at least to the 17th century"; and the tie between the Zweihänder, the title Master of the Long Sword and double pay: "no primary source confirms a direct connection, indicating that this link is likely a later myth." Doppelsöldner were front-rank volunteers, often with crossbow or arquebus.
    - *For 2d12:* a note only: knocking pikes aside and opening a breach has period support; "the pike-cutter on double pay for his sword" is the part historians doubt.
23. **The Parierhaken.** [checked, ARMA] The lugs "act almost as a secondary guard for the ricasso to catch and bind other weapons [...] They make up for the weapon's slowness on the defence and can allow another blade to be momentarily trapped". The developed two-hander "acquired the handling characteristics of a polearm rather than a sword [...] carried [...] across the shoulder like a pike or halberd" [checked, Wikipedia].
    - *For 2d12:* a note only: the big sword's second guard and its leather-wrapped ricasso are what let it be gripped short.
24. **The messer: one hand on the weapon, the other free to seize.** [read, Lecküchner] "you should fence with one hand with the Messer and you should have the other one on the back. If you want to fight with the empty hand, as in Messer-taking, grasping, clenching the arms etc., you should turn the hand from the back to the chest". A disarm: "go with the left hand to the 'strong' of his Messer and pull with both hands firmly to your left side, so that you take his Messer." The Nagel [checked, Wikipedia]: "a nail-like protrusion which projects out from the right side of the cross-guard [...] which protects the knuckles"; Lecküchner's text uses it actively ("mit dem wernagel stoß sein messer da mit hinweck": with the nail push his messer away) [read, the German; my gloss].
    - *For 2d12:* a note only: the messer's material is disarms and grabs with the off hand, and a guard that catches.
25. **The dusack and the sabre: the cut drilled as a pattern.** [read, Meyer] "all fencing is contained in these four cuts"; "before everything else, you would want to learn to cut the cuts long, free, and well"; he has them drilled in sequence "three or four [times] [...] through your opponent's face". For the sabre, the moulinet (the wrist's circular cut) is in Hutton and Waite, not read; a blog says of singlestick that "all guards, attacks, and moulinets with the sabre are equally applicable" [lead: search summary].
    - *For 2d12:* a note only: the cutting trainer's signature in the source is the flowing series of cuts, not a single trick.
26. **The estoc: a point for the gaps.** [checked, myArmoury] "The strong, rigid blade, designed for thrusting at armored opponents [...] there was a smooth, edgeless portion that enabled the user to grip the weapon with his other hand"; "especially suitable for blows aimed between the plates of armor and at opponents protected by mail". [checked, Wikipedia] "no cutting edge, just a point"; it "could also be used as lances once an actual lance had splintered".
    - *For 2d12:* a note only: the estoc is the half-sword thrust made into a whole weapon; it gives up the cut entirely.
27. **The katzbalger: the brawl after the pikes meet.** [checked] "a side-arm, often used by pikemen, archers, and crossbowmen as a last resort if the enemy were to draw too close"; "Mostly a cutting sword, the rounded tips on many examples are ill-suited to thrusting"; the likelier meaning of the name is "to fight at close quarters, to tussle". No period text on its use in the press was read.
    - *For 2d12:* a note only: its reputation is its name and its place (too close for the main weapon), not a recorded technique.
28. **Curved blades: the draw cut, forced by the hilt in the talwar's case.** [checked, Wikipedia] "The grip of the talwar is cramped and the prominent disc of the pommel presses into the wrist if attempts are made to use it to cut like a conventional sabre [...] enforcing the use of variations on the very effective 'draw cut'." The kilij's flared, sharpened back near the point (the yalman) and the shamshir's deep curve were not read in a source [lead].
    - *For 2d12:* a note only: the draw cut is the one technique a source ties to a curved sword's build.
29. **The falchion: weight forward, but a thin blade.** [checked, myArmoury] "a heavy single-edged blade [...] broadened considerably toward the point". [checked, Wikipedia] the cleaver type is "shaped very much like a large cleaver or machete", yet has "a very thin blade, often only 1.2 mm" at the spine near the point, ground "to a very acute edge". Silver [read] puts "Fauchions, Skaines, or Hangers" among the short weapons "of imperfect lengths" whose fight is "within the halfe or quarter Sword".
    - *For 2d12:* a note only: the surviving falchions are slicers of unarmoured flesh more than armour-breakers; "the chop" is its look.
30. **The rapier and the schiavona, in one line each.** Silver, against the rapier [read]: the thrusters "ran with their rapiers one at the other, & were both slaine"; the single sword "hath the vantage". The schiavona [checked, Wikipedia]: "While a rapier is primarily a thrusting sword, a schiavona is a cut and thrust sword that has extra weight", with a basket that covers the hand.
    - *For 2d12:* a note only: the period criticism of the rapier is the double kill, both fencers hit at once.
