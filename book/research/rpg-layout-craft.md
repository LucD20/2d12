# Layout craft: a rulebook that is serious and beautiful and still fast at the table

## 2026-11-16: for the book's look (the Action cards' "book feel")

**The question.** What do RPG layout designers, information designers and experienced reviewers say about laying out rules and ability blocks so that a book is both beautiful / serious and fast to use at the table? Covered: layout designers on their craft; atmosphere against usability; bold, italics, small caps and color in rules text; color coding and color blindness; icons against words; accessibility and readability facts; HTML rulebooks and SRD sites; designers' checklists for a block. `card-layout.md` already holds where the facts of a block sit (the roll line, grid against stacked lines): not repeated here.

**How far to trust this file.** One pass, by an agent (being filled in).

## Q1. RPG layout designers on their craft

**Explorers Design (Clayton Notestine)** [read: each essay on its page, with its date].
- *General Design 101* (Oct 2024): "Test your work by asking 'why?' [...] Have an answer that can be tied back to your project's big idea"; decisions add either "order, harmony, and affinity" or "chaos, tension, and contrast"; "order makes your design approachable and chaos makes it fresh". "Rules are relative. There's usually a path forward that doesn't require abandoning your creative concept or a design's usability."
- *Typography 101* (Nov 2024), adapted from Butterick: "Body Text. It's the foundation of every good book [...] start here." Size "10–12 points in print. Digitally, it's 15–25 pixels. In games, we tend to make our type too small or too big. When in doubt, compare your text to non-rpg books." Leading "120–145% of the point size". Line length "45–75 characters per line [...] in rpgs, our text is more mechanical and often benefits from paragraph breaks." "Your body text should be easy on the eyes, easy to read, and not too flashy. Let your game stand out with its mechanics, writing, and art. Don't make the text extreme to compensate." Tip: "Consider using one type family [...] you can focus on contrast by modifying the size and weight of your elements." The "Nohr Method": a grid of the same 800-character passage at every size and line height, printed, read, narrowed.
- *Mörk Borg (Layout Exhibit)* (May 2024): "Many describe it as chaotic, but our goal is to dispel that notion." "When in doubt, the next relevant piece of information is always to the right or below where your eye is." "Common regions [...] When multiple elements share a common region, they're perceived (and read) as a group." "There's a limit to how much deconstruction [...] an audience can take [...] This book has a grid system—it just happens to be after the rules and setting explanation." "Things like repetition, predictable rhythms, and patterns create stability, trust, and confidence in the reader. It's one of the core tenets of usability design". "space conveys information."
- *How to Design Signals in Books* (Dec 2025, from Jan V. White's *Editing by Design*): signals give "Distinction", "Orientation" ("Where am I?") and "Navigation". "Beware of false signals. Readers subconsciously look for patterns and can misinterpret decorations for signaling [...] if there is no meaning, it will confuse and even mislead your audience." "Use shared language between signals [...] Use identical words." "Design signals to match the visual identity [...] Use page numbers to reinforce your look and feel."
- *How to Design Lists in RPGs* (May 2026): a list "is 'loud'—designed to stand out". "Make items in the list structurally parallel." "attention-grabbing formatting is limited to the start of each successive bullet. Bold words in the middle of lists can make them harder to read." "For complicated lists, consider using lead-ins [...] with a bolder formatting. Think of your list like a series of ledes". Bullets "somewhere between 50% and 30% as big as the capital letter." Indent "one em".
- *False Dichotomies of RPG Design* (Apr 2025): "'Is your game hyper-usable or fun to read?' Well-written games can be fun to read and be usable." On white space: "It doesn't have to be filled with anything. Sometimes the emptiness is the point."

**Gavin Norman, Old-School Essentials** [read, his blog, Aug 2019]. "When I refer to 'the Old-School Essentials style', I'm not referring to the visual design of the books (e.g. the choice of fonts, table colours, etc.). Rather, I'm referring to the design principles that I used when writing, editing, and laying out the text. One of the most important OSE design principles is that each piece of content should fit onto a single page or spread, for the sake of usability [...] the reader will never have to flip a page back and forth". The spread also "serves as a limitation on class complexity".

**Paizo on Pathfinder 2e's playtest block** [read, Paizo blog "Attack the Stat Block" and the forum poll, May 2018]. The block is ordered by when it is read: "you're looking at the middle section when it's not the monster's turn, and at the bottom section on its turn"; a reaction sits "down here because the trigger is most likely to occur during the redcap's own turn." Erik Mona (publisher), in the poll thread: "I think even in-house we are skeptical that color is a useful way to delineate information, so that one is really on the fence [...] The symbols are a different story. We think they'll really help [...] Accessibility is a VERY important issue to us [...] the PRD and all 'text-based' versions of the rules will have the [A] and [R] designations in plain text".

**Other working designers** [checked, each on its page with its date].
- Graham Walmsley ("Doing simple layout", Aug 2023): "If there's one thing that makes a game look professional, it's a good typeface [...] Instead, use a classic typeface"; he warns against genre fonts ("a bad 'futuristic' typeface"). Margins as simple fractions of the page; paragraph spacing a fraction or multiple of the type size; a footnote credits Jason Morningstar: "x-height matters more than the size itself."
- Golem Productions (Sabrina Jatscha, a graphic designer, Mar 2025): "There is one layout rule that stands above all else: It must be easy to use at the table!"; they aim "to fit all the necessary information to run it on a single spread." Their first draft "was essentially just text—no fonts, no colors, no highlights".
- James Maliszewski (Grognardia, Feb 2026): "Maybe the trick is not to aim for either 'professional' or 'primitive,' but for appropriate. A roleplaying book should be clear, usable, and inviting. It should look like it was produced for play, not for display." "Bad layout, illegible type, muddy photocopies, and willfully crude artwork are not inherently virtuous [...] they just make it harder to read."
- Welcome to the Deathtrap (Brian Rideout, Aug 2021), on adventures: "Hail the Mighty Spread [...] avoid making the reader have to flip pages, especially in the middle of a paragraph"; "a good layout and information design can vastly improve the play experience. Especially if not overdone."
- **Brennen Reece** (Bully Pulpit Games' designer, interviewed Mar 2017) [checked]: "RPGs are first teaching texts, and then reference texts, so I'd start there. There are some incredibly well designed books for art instruction, bushcraft, cooking, woodworking, knitting". On the 1983 Red Box: "There aren't any weird typefaces, obnoxious fake parchment page backgrounds, eldritch page borders. The typography is pretty damned competent." His "mid-century formalism": "clean, crisp typography, stripped of ornamentation [...] concerned with communicating information rather than decorating it." From Bringhurst: "the design should honor the text, and the reading experience shouldn't be made more difficult by poor craftsmanship and visual distractions"; designers "use a very small palette of typefaces". From Tufte: "Anything that isn't contributing to the signal is contributing to the noise." "I have no problem suggesting an edit to fit the text on the page."
- **The Gauntlet forum, "How To Make RPG Books More Readable?"** (Apr 2019, an archived copy) [checked]: two-page spreads ("I don't want to have to flip pages to continue reading something"), "a really good index", PDF links "to find the definition of a move". "White space. Most RPG pages are far too crowded"; "Concision. Cut the text in half"; "Since the books serves at least two masters (learning and reference) a perfect organization is probably impossible. So repeat necessary text rather than reference it." Another: "any game text faces a dilemma between two objectives [...] The aim to teach the game and the aim to support play [...] There is a reason that Fantasy Flight is providing two rulebooks with many of their board games". A designer's list: "A clear visual hierarchy [...] Simplicity before graphics effects [...] Be consistent in your layout and design."
- **The other side: "sterile" and "art book"** [checked]. Ryan Howard (RGH Press, Mar 2024): "Move too far into the realm of informational presentation and you have a text book or something sterile like Old-School Essentials. Move too far the other way and you have something like [...] Cy_Borg which has completely sacrificed legibility for the sake of the aesthetic"; "A proper RPG book should be designed to be useful. It is a reference book at the end of the day"; his model is the DK Eyewitness book (a central image, a lead paragraph, labeled details). Malmuria (Familiar Waves, Aug 2022): OSE "is obviously the standard bearer [...] While some say it lacks the charm of the original, it more than makes up for it in efficient, 'control panel' two page spreads"; Mörk Borg's "layout and art in the core book conveys its tone non-verbally [...] the parts that need to be well organized are, exceptionally so". Orthopraxy (Jan 2026), answering Ben Milton's video against Goodman Games' dense layout: Winter's Daughter "was written not for the reader, but for the active GM"; a novel-like module can be "eaten" and remembered so the book is not needed at the table.
- **Johan Nohr** (Mörk Borg) [checked, Grognardia's interview, Dec 2020]: "we basically wanted to see what would happen if we broke a lot of rules and challenged a lot of norms when it comes to book design [...] It's still a fully playable game, mind you, but the art aspect is important." Explorers Design notes "a bare-bones edition of Mörk Borg for people with accessibility difficulties" [read].
- **EN World, "Graphic Design Layout Artists: The Reason TTRPGs Look Great"** (Jul 2024) [checked]: a layout artist quoted in the article: "I shouldn't have to dismantle your work completely to uncover stats [...] It's incredibly vexing to find myself resorting to using a highlighter". Replies: "having black background makes things harder to read [...] I might be less inclined to purchase"; "White background, great black and white art, readable font and I am happy"; "attractive layouts are nice, useful layouts are necessary [...] Information architecture is insanely important"; "Even very structured design can still have a ton of character."
- **Draw Steel's book** [checked, EN World, Jun 2026]: against: "the lack of modern layouting tools like boxes, highlighting etc. It feels like this was just a word document made to PDF". For: "the issues with layout are overblown -- at east as they relate to class layout. It COULD use a little color or other design flourishes, but the abilities are actually pretty well laid out in practice"; "The rules portion where it's big blocks of text is where I struggle!"; "The text is smaller than necessary". Gaming Trend's review (2025) [checked]: the keyword definitions are "essential stuff, but it is presented rather dryly"; "a whole lot of book flipping"; one tab color for the whole classes third of the book "feels like a missed chance". No designer's own account of the ability block's look was found.

## Q6. Accessibility and readability: what the sources actually say

- **British Dyslexia Association, *Dyslexia friendly style guide*** [read, a copy of the BDA page (the 2023 guide) kept by Boston University; the BDA's own page loaded without its text]. Fonts: "Use sans serif fonts, such as Arial and Comic Sans [...] Alternatives include Verdana, Tahoma, Century Gothic, Trebuchet, Calibri, Open Sans." Size "12-14 point or equivalent (e.g. 1-1.2em / 16-19 px)". Line spacing "1.5/150% is preferable." "Avoid underlining and italics as this can make the text appear to run together and cause crowding. Use bold for emphasis." "Avoid using all capital letters [...] for continuous text." Headings "at least 20% larger than the normal text." Colour: "Use dark coloured text on a light (not white) background"; "Avoid green and red/pink"; "Use cream or a soft pastel colour"; "use matt paper rather than gloss". Layout: "Left align text, without justification"; "Avoid multiple columns (as used in newspapers)"; "Write short simple sentences: 60 to 70 characters is optimal" (the guide puts the number on sentences, not line length); "Use white space to remove clutter near text and group related content." "Pictograms and graphics can help to locate and support information in the text."
- **Serif against sans: the controlled study finds no difference at reading sizes.** Arditi and Cho, *Serifs and font legibility*, Vision Research 2005 [read]: "RSVP and continuous reading speeds showed no effect of serifs. When text is small or distant, serifs may, then, produce a tiny legibility increase due to the concomitant increase in spacing. However, our data exhibited no difference in legibility between typefaces that differ only in the presence or absence of serifs." "unlikely to be of significance at typical print sizes viewed under normal conditions." Their own caveats: one font of their own design, a small sample.
- **Dyslexia and fonts** [quoted: Rello and Baeza-Yates, *Good fonts for dyslexia*, ASSETS 2013, 48 readers with dyslexia, eye-tracking; read through BYU's Editing Research summary, May 2020]: "special fonts like OpenDyslexic [...] were not, in fact, easier to read, but fonts like Arial and Helvetica were. Helvetica and Courier specifically led to shorter fixation durations"; "italics versions of these fonts proved to be harder to read". The paper itself was not opened.
- **WCAG 2.2** [read]. 1.4.1 Use of Color: "Color is not used as the only visual means of conveying information, indicating an action, prompting a response, or distinguishing a visual element." 1.4.3 Contrast (Minimum): "at least 4.5:1", "Large-scale text [...] at least 3:1"; "18 point text or 14 point bold text is judged to be large".
- **Color vision deficiency** [read, the US National Eye Institute]: "About 1 in 12 men have color vision deficiency"; "The most common type [...] makes it hard to tell the difference between red and green. Another type makes blue and yellow look the same." (1 in 12 is the "about 8%".)
