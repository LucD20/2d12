# Talents: the design thinking that cuts across games (raw report)

**Date:** 2026-11-19

**Lucas's words:** "The talents is going to be the actual meat of the game, so far we have been doing the skeleton. Talents is going to be the main thing, how things work, how all of it comes together. How a diplomatic paladin differs from a quiet, ranged paladin, with similar strengths and capabilities. I don't want to fall into the trap of skill feats from 2e, or skill tax from 1e and 3.5, or competing talents like edges from Swade (why nobody really gets social edges instead of combat edges)."

**Scope.** The arguments designers make about character options, across games. Not a catalogue of systems (three other reports cover the d20 feats, the free picks and the trees). **Nothing here designs 2d12's Talents.**

**What was read first.** Chapter 5 (the level list, "Levels give options; Stages change the numbers", Swapping on a Rest); `rules-src/set-aside/16-talents.md`; rules-backlog.md, "TALENT STRUCTURE: PROPOSAL"; `roleplay-skills.md` (all); `class-charter.md` (all); `archive/book-drafts/options-talents.md` (skimmed); `magic-design.md` decisions 113 to 118 and 121, sections 3.2 and 5l; `research/README.md`, `nova-burst.md`, `progression-feel.md`, `magic-vs-skills.md` (not repeated here).

## 1. What a character option is for

- **Expression first.** Sid Meier's list of what makes a decision interesting ends on the personal one: "This interesting decision would allow you to express your personal play style" [read, reported by Game Developer]. Rosewater: "You're simply more attached to things that you had a hand in creating. Customization isn't just a way to crowd-source content, it's a means to give players the tools to make your game personal", and "Your players don't need to love everything, but they need to love something" [read].
- **Players love the idea of options more than the options.** Greg Street, on seven years of talent trees: "When players talk about their love of options, I think what they are really saying is they are in love with the idea of having dozens of interesting talents. We just don't think that will ever happen" [read].
- **Build against character.** Mage Hand Press: optimized builds converge ("character diversity tends to suffer when the more optimized builds start looking the same"), and "a character should not suffer mechanically just because a player doesn't want to play Great Weapon Fighter #49" [checked (s)]. A Draw Steel commentator: "the purpose of a skill system is to encourage character expression"; Draw Steel's skills are "tools for character expression" that "aren't necessary (or expected) for players to achieve success" [checked (s)].
- **Separating the three.** The cleanest separations found: 13th Age's One Unique Thing is expression with capability forbidden: "Your character's unique should not provide general practical value in combat. That is not the intent. The intent is to open up story arcs and fun roleplaying opportunities" [read]. Richard Baker, who wrote the first 3e combat feats: "I always felt the best feats were talents or tricks you could describe without numbers if you had to" [read]: capability stated as fiction, power left out. A feat as identity: Mearls's 2013 column is described as coming "close to 'feats are Fate-like Aspects'", a pick being "a firm declaration about who your character is" [checked (s); Mearls's text not opened].
- **What players regret.** Picks that never come up (section 5's thread: "it's really hard to come up with scenarios in which having the feat would do anything" [checked]); picks that were secretly required (section 3); a pick that cost them the ability to keep up in a fight (section 4). The Sawyer talk's standard is the one that speaks to regret: "Viable: your character can survive the game; Optimal: how far a min/maxer will push a character", and the aim "to reduce the gap between viable and optimal" [read, Soren Johnson's notes of Sawyer's talk].

## 2. Horizontal growth: a wider sheet nobody remembers

(`progression-feel.md` already holds "it's really hard to have horizontal progression and not constantly increase complexity". New here:)

- **Complexity is a budget, and tracking is the worst kind.** Rosewater names three kinds: comprehension ("understanding what a card does"), board ("how they interact with one another") and strategic ("how best to use your cards"); "Comprehension complexity and board complexity are a problem for beginners. Strategic complexity is not." And: "By bookkeeping, I mean the requirement to pay attention to elements of the game that could matter but often don't. In general, bookkeeping is something you want to take out of games" [read]. A conditional passive is exactly that bookkeeping.
- **Lenticular design** is his answer to depth without load: an option whose surface is simple ("it has to appear to do something") and whose depth is strategic, invisible until the player is ready [read].
- **Daggerheart's designers write the limit into the format.** "Limit the cognitive load": the design "is mindful of the number of additional mechanics and modifiers the player has to keep in mind at any given time"; "classes shouldn't include too many stacking mechanics"; "A player shouldn't need to refer to a book to use their character's features"; "If a rule doesn't fit in the space allotted, it's probably not right for Daggerheart" [read; the kit is by Gage, Hall, Starke and Underwood]. The game then caps what is live: five cards in the loadout, the rest in the vault, a Recall Cost as "the price for versatility" [read]. So breadth owned and breadth in play are separated. A critic of the cards: re-sorting them each rest is "The Loadout Shuffle", and checking card by card is "just slower than scanning a character sheet" [checked (s)].
- **Reactions and triggers load memory hardest.** The same kit, on stat blocks: "be aware of adding too many reactions, as these tend to be the heaviest passive cognitive load" [read].
- **What actually gets forgotten.** Skerples, after playtesting dozens of GLOG classes: the Thief's "Heist" was never used, the Wizard's "Spell Breeding" rarely, the Knight's "Challenge" underused; "Player enjoyment seems to come mostly from the weird interactions between treasure, abilities, enemies, factions, and traps, not from any one ability a class has" [read]. 4e players report conditional bonuses "forgotten constantly" on characters built at high level and manageable when built up gradually [lead]; 5e's designers are said to have cut "rarely-meaningful and frequently-forgotten pluses and minuses" [lead, DM David].
- **How many.** No designer gave a tested number. Forum figures: "four or five options at once" (EN World) [checked (s)]; "four is a good number" for situational abilities; "10 available actions" as one player's ceiling [lead]. Treat as folk estimates.
- **Always-on against buttons.** Always-on numbers are forgotten least and felt least (13th Age's second edition calls its old high-tier feats "small math-bumps" [read]); always-on rule changes are remembered when they change a decision; buttons are remembered when few and cost attention when many. No source measured this: it is the pattern across the complaints.

## 3. Traps, taxes, must-haves

- **Ivory Tower, as far as it can be read.** Cook's essay was not opened. As quoted by the Alexandrian [read, of the quotations]: "Toughness, for example, has its uses, but in most cases it's not the best choice of feat"; it could have been written to say it was for "1st-level elf wizards (where it is likely to give them a 100 percent increase in hit points)". Alexander's reading: Cook's regret was the missing instructions ("we probably should have included more instructions on when those tools are useful"), not a confession of planted traps; Toughness "is a special purpose tool" [checked].
- **The repudiation.** Sawyer: "Wanted no bad builds on Pillars, everything should be viable" and "If something is required for viable play, WHY make it an option" [read, via Johnson's notes]. Introcaso states the same rule for Draw Steel: "When something becomes an inevitability, right, like [...] you should always use XYZ character option, we then try to make that either built-in or bring up the other options to be comparable" [read]. Street on the alternative: "bloated trees that have a ton of inconsequential talents that you have to slog through to get to the fun stuff" [read].
- **The counter-argument.** Alexander: demanding all options be equal is "like demanding that a2-a3 and the King's Gambit both be equally valid openings in Chess"; his "naturalistic balance" accepts weak choices because meaningful choice requires that a poor one is possible [checked; the second (s)]. Rosewater's defense of weak cards: "R&D purposefully slopes the cards to allow a constant sense of discovery", "Different cards have different functions and appeal to different players", and the thrill of finding the dismissed card [read]. **Why it transfers badly:** a Magic player owns hundreds of cards and replays; a tabletop player owns one character for a year. A critic on the Sawyer side still has a point worth keeping: if nothing is bad, "what stops player from clicking through chargen screens with their eyes closed" [lead].
- **Definitions that several sources share.** A **tax**: "A required feat that designers introduce in order to make up for a design oversight" (EN World; 4e's Expertise feats are the standard case) [lead]; Robert Schwalb is reported sorting 4e's feats into skill, math, race/class and power feats and condemning the math ones [checked (s); his post not opened]. A **must-have**: Soren Johnson, "a single, dominant strategy actually takes away choice from a game because all other options are provably sub-optimal" [read]. A **trap**: an option whose surface value is above its real value, in a game where the choice is hard to undo (Alexander's "hidden traps that require system mastery" [checked (s)]).
- **Opportunity cost is the price.** "Selecting any skill (or class feature, advantage, etc.) has an opportunity cost; the player who chooses it forfeits other talents" [checked, a guest author on Monte Cook Games' site].
- **The test for an interesting choice.** Johnson: "The sweet spot for game design is when a specific decision is right in some circumstances but not in others" [read]. Meier: "Good decisions are situational" [read]. Street gives the working version: "Choosing between a talent that grants 10% damage and one that grants 5% damage [...] isn't interesting"; 5% haste against 5% crit "more than likely [has] a right answer (and like most of us, you'll probably just ask someone else what the answer is)"; "a root or a snare can be interesting. Which does more damage? Hard to say. Which is better? It depends on the situation" [read]. And his test method: "It is 'easy' [...] to determine the value of a DPS talent [...]. It is hard to determine the DPS value of Improved Sprint or Lichborne" [read]: options that can't be put on one scale can't be solved.
- **The limit of that method.** Johnson: "Given the opportunity, players will optimize the fun out of a game", and "designers don't even understand their own games until they finally see them in the wild" [read; second (s)]. A commentator on Street's redesign predicted it would only move the cookie-cutter to one build per encounter [lead].

## 4. Combat and non-combat options in one budget

**Why non-combat loses** (each stated by someone found):
1. **Stakes.** Combat is "the largest (and sometimes only) circumstance in which your character could die", so "every non-combat ability you pick has an additional opportunity cost in that you didn't pick a combat ability" (Cannibal Halfling) [checked]. "A botched Defense roll may kill your character and end the game"; a failed conversation only angers someone (Rampant Coyote, of computer RPGs) [checked (s)].
2. **Nothing under it.** "3E lacks the supporting mechanisms around 'social' or roleplaying feats that make them truly work" (EN World, 2002) [checked (s)]. A non-combat option needs a rule to bite on.
3. **The table plays it without dice.** If key social scenes are always roleplayed, social skills "quickly become irrelevant or, at best, something used to avoid minor nuisances" (MCG guest article) [checked].
4. **Frequency is the campaign's, not the designer's.** Designers "can't assume how much each campaign splits between combat and non-combat" (MerricB, 2007) [checked (s)]; Alexander: a fighter "is less useful" in a campaign with little combat, so equal concepts need either a narrow game or narrow characters [checked (s)].
5. **Letting the party down.** Players avoid weaker options because they want to contribute; most options become "not real" in practice (Playthings of Mad Gods) [checked (s)].
6. **One specialist is enough outside a fight, everyone fights.** [memory; 2d12's decision 117 (the best suited PC rolls) sharpens it.]

**The fixes, with what was found for and against:**

| Fix | For | Against |
|---|---|---|
| **Siloed slots** (4e's attack / utility / ritual; Pathfinder 2e's skill feats; 13th Age's background points; Draw Steel's perks) | "balancing combat effectiveness against non-combat effectiveness is a fool's errand"; "You need three silos and you shouldn't be able trade them off" (EN World) [checked (s)]. | The wall leaks: the liked skill feats are "combat buffs in disguise [...] They're all great, but they're basically class feats" [checked]. Some players want the trade: "They want the ability to make that trade-off" [checked (s)]. A silo with nothing under it is filler ("4E seemed to say forget it, the only thing worth having systems for is combat") [checked (s)]. "Trying to hard code it into the game starting at chargen is a mistake" [checked (s)]. |
| **A second currency** | The same argument as silos. | The backlog already records point-buy's second pools being min-maxed [project]. |
| **Both halves in every option** | Proposed for D&D Next's feats (each feat touching combat, exploration, interaction) [checked (s)]. | "it being baked into each individual feat is a worse approach"; it adds dependencies [checked (s)]. Players read only the combat half [memory]. Daggerheart's grimoire guidance shows the pricing knot: one strong combat spell beside "two useful but more moderate utility spells" [read]. |
| **Free, or from background** (13th Age's Unique and backgrounds; 5e's ribbons; Draw Steel's career perk) | No competition at all. 13th Age forbids the Unique any "general practical value in combat" [read]. | Forgettable ribbons (already in `roleplay-skills.md`); free-form ones are hard to bound. |
| **Make the non-combat side mechanically real** | Introcaso: people now answer "Is Draw Steel just combat?" with "there's this negotiation system, there's montages, and there's downtime" [read]. | "The roll replacing play" and the sheet as a cage (`roleplay-skills.md`). The negotiation system "did not always work well" in playtest [read]. |
| **Advancement by use** | Removes the purchase. | Grinding (`mastery-progression.md`); Johnson's Morrowind example of players wasting their own time [checked (s)]. |
| **Options keyed to a situation, not a mode** | "an artificial, metagame distinction": Balance or a ranged attack serve either mode (EN World) [checked (s)]. Draw Steel's perks: "many are versatile enough that they can also be used in battle" [lead]. | Dagredhel's reply: "if noncombat choices feed into combat, players will pick them only for combat benefit" [checked (s)]: the option is then priced, and chosen, as a combat option. |
| **Let the class or subclass carry it** | Daggerheart's Syndicate rogue: "roleplay-focused", so "some battle-inclined players may not give it a second glance [...] The point of creating subclasses is to provide a variety of options for different players" [read]. | It accepts that some players never take it: a choice of taste, not a solution. |

**The evidence is thin everywhere.** No source had numbers on what players pick. The best single observation is the Pathfinder 2e one: a separate pool did not stop the pool's best picks from being the combat ones.

## 5. Permission

- **The sharpest statement.** Papers & Pencils (2011): "some feats allow characters to perform tasks which they should be able to perform whether or not they have a feat"; "Anytime something which should be available to all players becomes a feat, it arbitrarily steals that ability from everyone who doesn't take the feat"; the good kind "improve characters which take them, but do not imply a restriction upon characters which do not" [checked].
- **The mermaid.** The core book shows mermaids breathing air and water; a supplement adds a power that lets a mermaid breathe air, so now they couldn't. As ThePiachu frames it: "a mechanic that solves a problem you didn't know you had"; advice: "maybe it's best not to solve problems nobody is having" [checked]. His own case is languages: a "speaks all languages" playbook means either one player talks to every alien or the table ignores the rule.
- **Why it keeps happening.** Staffan (EN World, 2025): Pathfinder 2e "reduces the scope for basic skills, in order for Skill Feats to have something to do" [checked]. **A large list of small options creates permission by itself:** each needs a scrap of ground, and the ground comes from the base rule. Cannibal Halfling says the same of all list-based design: it excludes what is not on the list [checked (s)]. Mustrum_Ridcully: the problem predates 2e ("existed with feats in 3rd Edition") [checked (s)].
- **The designers' own escape hatches.** Draw Steel prints one: "The existence of specific perks doesn't mean that a hero can't attempt the task related to a perk without having that perk. Aside from supernatural perks, a Director can always allow a hero to attempt a mundane task mentioned in a perk by making a test. Perks are special because they allow a hero to attempt a specific task without a test"; its example is catching a falling ally: without the perk, a Might test and an action to prepare; "Being able to catch an ally automatically as a free triggered action is what the perk gets you!" [read]. Mark Seifter (reposted from Reddit): judge an improvised action "by its own merit", not by hunting for a feat; but a feat as a gate can also protect another skill's niche [checked (s)]. Mearls's 2013 principle, as reported: a feat should not close the door on a character without it trying the same thing "as a one-off stunt", "only intermittently observed" since [checked (s)].
- **The forms that add without forbidding** (collected from the above): you do X **without a roll** (Draw Steel); you do X **faster or as a lesser action** (the catch as a free triggered action); **when you do X, also Y**; **a floor** ("Tier 1 outcomes on easy or medium tests with that skill count as tier 2": Draw Steel's Area of Expertise [read (s)]); **X at a scale nobody assumes** (the thread's model feats: "New utility options that exist on the edge of realism. Wall jumping doesn't exactly work in real life, so I wouldn't assume every character could do it" [checked]).
- **Where permission is fine.** The supernatural (Draw Steel's "Aside from supernatural perks"); the superhuman (gating "makes a lot more sense for a supers or a high fantasy game" [checked (s)]); niche protection, on purpose (Seifter).
- **The cost of the escape hatch.** "Some DMs can decide you CANNOT do the thing (if you ever could) without the feat" (Seramus: "DM May I?") [checked (s)]: a sentence in the GM chapter does not bind the table the way the option's own wording does.

## 6. Two characters of one class

- **The complaint to avoid:** "Two first level rogues are likely to be a lot alike" (Mike Shea on 13th Age, 2013) [checked (s)]. Tom Bloom, on Draw Steel: "there's so few choices you're actually making when it comes to it, that it seems a little stiff to me" [read (s)].
- **A second axis that isn't the class.** Introcaso: "Kits allow you to feel very different, but really customize your equipment, narratively" [read]. Draw Steel stacks ancestry, culture, career, class, kit, and its publisher's claim is that "even two heroes of the same class and ancestry can be very different" [lead]. Daggerheart: a class is two domains, a subclass is "the more specific ways a class might be played", and domain cards must be "Class-Neutral" [read]. 13th Age puts the difference in what the player writes (the Unique, backgrounds as "broad categories of experience (cat burglar, for example)" that "don't sync to a specific ability score") [read]. Cypher's sentence (an adjective, a noun, a verb) is the "what you are × how you do it" grid in its purest form [memory].
- **How much the class should fix.** Street's conclusion after the trees: players "want to choose their spec based on flavor [...] or rotation"; "You should pick a spec because you like the rotation or the kit" [read]: the class (or spec) fixes the loop of play, the picks vary around it. Mage Hand Press's test for a new option: it should "add to a character concept without overwhelming the existing options" [checked (s)].
- **Suggestions on top of freedom.** Teos Abadia (Alphastream, 2013): three tiers of presentation, "One Default Option", "Several Simple Options", "Many Complex Options", with the default "simple enough for anyone to pick up"; the tiers share one table because "The game isn't played by new and casual people at one table and experienced hard-core gamers at another"; and "If we let the game be dominated by feats, the game will drown [...] Make them the flourish" [checked (s)]. That last line is the opposite of Lucas's stated plan (Talents as the meat): see (d).

## 7. The structure of the choosing

- **Few that matter against many.** Street: "You will have fewer choices. But you will have more choices that *matter*"; and why the big matrix can't be saved: "The problem is the extreme number of combinations. When you have such a gigantic matrix, the chances of having unbeatable synergies, or combinations of talents that just don't work together is really high. That's not lazy design. That is recognizing how math works"; "we don't think it's humanly possible to have 40-50 fun, interesting and balanced talents in a tree" [read]. Richard Baker on the other end: 3e grew to "literally thousands of feats [...] far too many to manage", and 5e's cut to one per four levels was "a bit of an overcorrection" [read]. Mearls in 2017 on why 5e went no further: "Been somewhat surprised at lack of enthusiasm for them. Not negative, dislike design, more just not using" [read (s)].
- **A small hand against a catalogue.** Street's rows of three; Daggerheart's two domains at a level; the roguelike "one of three" (no designer's reason found [lead]). The argument for the hand is section 3's: the comparison is possible. The argument against is section 6's stiffness.
- **Tiers that feel different.** Heinsoo, 13th Age 2e: "Talents that didn't measure up have been downgraded into class feats. Feats that were originally epic feats now frequently appear as adventurer-tier feats, with champion-tier and epic-tier feats above them that truly feel epic instead of like small math-bumps"; the goal, "to give every character more interesting choices" [read]. Daggerheart sets the ceiling in words: level 10 is "legendary heroes without reaching the power of gods" [read].
- **Chains.** Avoiding feat chains is praised as "one of the best decisions in the whole design" of 5e (Stoddard) [checked (s)]. A chain is a tax paid in advance.
- **Synergy against combo tax.** Daggerheart wants cards that "synergize internally" and across domains, with a guard: "No one domain should be so strong that characters would be incentivized to take cards exclusively from that domain"; yet its level 7 "X-Touched" cards reward four or more cards of one domain [read]: the same book pulls both ways.
- **Respec.** Street draws a line by reason: "Asking a player to swap from damage to tanking for a couple of fights is acceptable to us. Asking someone to respec from Unholy to Frost just for the debuff is not" [read]: free swapping is fine until the group starts demanding the swap. Tim Cain (not opened): respec helps experimentation, cheapens decisions, and can turn "bad design into a player problem" [lead]. Daggerheart prices it in play (Recall Cost) and frees it at rest [read]. Pathfinder 2e's retraining takes downtime; Lancer lets a pilot rebuild between missions [memory].
- **Cards as a format.** For: a text limit that forces plain rules, tangibility, easy side-by-side browsing at creation ("an intuitive browsing experience that pure text lists can't match"). Against: table space, slower than a sheet in play [read for the kit; checked (s) for the critic].

## 8. Options that modify an action against options that add one

Little designer argument was found; what there is:

- **For modifying.** Abadia: feats should "extend what they already do" [checked (s)]. 13th Age attaches its feats to a talent or a power, so growth lands where the player already looks ("feats built to boost specific powers") [checked (s)]. No new button competes for the turn.
- **Against.** The modifier lives away from the thing it modifies, so it is the forgotten kind unless it is written into the action (Rosewater's bookkeeping; the 4e reports in section 2). Stacked riders are the "stacking mechanics" Daggerheart warns classes away from [read]. And the cheap rider is the "small math-bump" [read].
- **For adding.** A new verb is felt and remembered, and it is what Baker's "describe without numbers" test passes most easily [read].
- **Against adding.** Each new button is one more thing in the hand (section 2) and one more thing the base rule can't already let everyone do (section 5).
- **On the sheet.** Daggerheart, 4e and Draw Steel print the whole ability in one place; none found prints a rider apart from its action and is praised for it [memory, from the layouts in `card-layout.md`].

## 9. Options keyed on traits or tags

- **What it buys.** One option touches many abilities without naming them, and new abilities join by carrying the tag. Daggerheart's domains are this: "thematic threads that run through the features, providing cohesion and allowing for synergies and combos within the domain" [read].
- **Parasitic design.** Rosewater's term, as reported: a parasitic mechanic is "making use of a resource found only in the set it is in" [lead], the "opposite of backwards-compatible mechanics" [checked (s)]. A Goblin Artisans designer: parasitic cards are "linear cards that only 'play nice' with other cards from their set or block"; a wider key would have kept Kamigawa's splice cards in play; yet "Parasitism is sometimes a necessary evil" [checked (s)]. **For a tabletop option:** a Talent keyed to a tag only one class carries is a class feature under another name; a tag carried by many things makes the option general.
- **Linear against modular** (Rosewater, through others): a linear option tells you what to build around it; a modular one fits anywhere [lead]. Linear options make builds obvious (good for a new player, a cookie-cutter for an old one).
- **Keyword soup.** Nothing by a designer was found under that name. The nearest: New World Order's point that comprehension complexity is paid by every reader of every card [read], and the Pathfinder 2e thread's feats that "interact with time in a way that would never come up" [checked]: a tag or a subsystem nobody uses makes every option keyed to it dead.

## 10. Writing and balancing a large set

- **Size.** Street: 40 to 50 balanced, interesting talents in one tree is not "humanly possible" [read]. Cannibal Halfling: "if your list is several hundred items long, though, it's not good design!" (Exalted's 784 Solar Charms) [checked (s)]. A blog on subclasses: three, because six to eight "are never given the space" [lead]. Heinsoo admits the first 13th Age core had classes with "a few great choices and a significant number of meh choices" because "we didn't entirely know what we were doing yet" [read; the second (s)].
- **Pricing.** The numbers found are already in the project (Heinsoo's 1 : 1.5 : 2 in `nova-burst.md`). New: Sean K. Reynolds's feat points, as forum posts describe them, priced feats unequally (Toughness 3, Weapon Focus 7) and noted "specialization is rewarded" and that a feat's worth differs by class [lead]; critics answered that Weapon Focus and Skill Focus "involve different parts of the system" and can't share a scale [lead]. Daggerheart scales by the range wanted: tier (1 to 4) for strong features, a trait or Proficiency for 1 to 6, level for 1 to 10 [read]. Nobody found states "a pick is worth X% of a turn".
- **Templates and threads without filler.** Daggerheart: each domain has "several thematic threads", "low-level cards establishing the thread and higher-level cards building on that thread" [read]. Rosewater's cycles do the same job [memory]. The filler test is Street's: a talent you "slog through to get to the fun stuff" [read].
- **Finding dominated options.** Introcaso's rule (make it built-in or raise the others) [read]; Sawyer's "reality check" from watching what players dump [read, via Johnson]; Johnson: only the wild shows it [read].
- **Roleplay options need a frequency.** Daggerheart: "If they can use the feature too frequently, the story's pace not only slows down too often, but the roleplay moments also lose their emotional weight", so many are once a session or once a rest [read].
- **Creep.** Baker's "thousands"; Schwalb's reported argument that proliferation hurt 4e [checked (s)]; `magic-vs-skills.md`'s "it drifts back". Street's structural answer is a fixed grid: a new talent must replace one.

## 11. Non-combat options that are loved

The evidence is the thinnest of the report: few named examples, no survey.

- **Named with approval.** Pathfinder 2e: Wall Jump, Legendary Thief, Legendary Survivalist ("on the edge of realism") and Improvise Tool; the liked but combat ones: Battle Medicine, Bon Mot, Titan Wrestler [checked; Improvise Tool a lead]. Draw Steel: I've Got You (the catch, automatic) [read]. Daggerheart's designers' own examples of "explicitly roleplay-oriented" cards: Rune Ward, A Soldier's Bond, Reassurance, which "ask the player to perform some interpersonal interaction and/or to do worldbuilding in the moment, creating a mechanical effect that corresponds to the fiction created by the player" [read]. 13th Age's One Unique Thing [read]. A stolen spell one must "find some creative way to use" [lead].
- **Named as dead.** "+2/+2 feats are incredibly sterile" (EN World, on D&D Next) [checked (s)]; "I don't want flat numerical bonuses like skill focus, run, or endurance" (2002) [checked (s)]; counting items at a glance, skim reading, impressing two people at once [checked].
- **What the loved ones share.** (1) They make a scene or a moment at the table (Daggerheart: "it sells the importance of the mechanical effect by requiring that the character creates a moment in the story to benefit from it"). (2) They are reliable: no roll, or a floor. (3) They are the character's alone, often written by the player. (4) They are not a number. (5) They sit just past what anyone could do. (6) They are rationed when they slow play.

## What seems to transfer

### (a) Principles several independent designers state

1. **An option is worth having when it is right in some situations and not in others.** Johnson, Meier, Street. *Counter:* situational options are the ones that go unused or forgotten (Skerples; the "would never come up" feats); "situational" needs the situation to occur at this table.
2. **If everyone must take it, build it in.** Sawyer, Introcaso, the 4e Expertise complaint. *Counter:* Alexander and Rosewater: unequal options are what make choosing a skill, and "no bad builds" can mean no choice that matters.
3. **Fewer choices that can be compared beat many that can't.** Street, Baker, Abadia, Daggerheart's text limit. *Counter:* Baker's "overcorrection"; Bloom's "stiff"; Mearls's players who simply didn't use feats when feats were few and optional.
4. **A pick should be describable without numbers.** Baker; 13th Age 2e's "math-bumps"; the "sterile" +2s; Street's root against snare. *Counter:* numbers are the easiest options to remember, price and stack; a flat-number game with only verbs gets wide (`progression-feel.md`).
5. **An option must not take from everyone else what the base rule gave.** Papers & Pencils, the mermaid, Draw Steel's box, Mearls (reported), Seifter. *Counter:* Seifter's niche protection; and the supernatural, where gating is the point.
6. **Combat and non-combat picks should not be bought from one pool.** 4e's silos, Pathfinder 2e, 13th Age, the EN World threads. *Counter:* the wall leaks unless it is also a wall on content; some players want the trade; a silo with no rules under it is filler.
7. **Memory is the budget: limit what is live, and above all what triggers off-turn or conditionally.** Rosewater, Daggerheart, the 4e reports. *Counter:* the loadout shuffle; a cap on live options is itself a rule to manage.
8. **You only learn which options are dead or dominant from play.** Johnson, Sawyer, Skerples, Heinsoo. *Counter:* none found; it bounds how far pricing on paper can go.

### (b) Tests for a single Talent

- **Tax:** would a character who skips it fall below what the game's numbers assume? Does it fix something the base rule should? If nearly everyone of a kind would take it, it is a feature.
- **Trap:** does it read stronger than it plays? Does its text say when it is the right tool (Cook's own regret)? How often will its situation occur in a session?
- **Must-have:** is there a build where it is not the best pick in its slot? Can it be put on one scale with its neighbors (if yes, one of them wins)?
- **Permission:** before this Talent existed, would a GM have let anyone try this? If yes, it must be written as better, faster, without a roll, a floor, or an added result. Does it need the base rule narrowed to have room?
- **Bare number:** can it be described without a number? Does it change a decision or a visible moment?
- **Forgettable:** is it a condition to remember on someone else's turn or on a roll that already happened? Is it printed where it is used? Is it "could matter but often doesn't"?
- **Parasitic:** how many things carry the tag it keys on, and will that count grow?
- **Scene:** for a non-combat Talent: does using it create a moment, is it reliable, and does it need a frequency?

### (c) Tests for the structure

- **Two kinds of play in one budget?** Is any single pick ever a choice between fight and not-fight? If slots are separate, can a pick in the non-combat slot be used in a fight (the leak), and is there a rule under every non-combat pick for it to bite on?
- **How many decisions?** Picks per level, options per pick, and whether two options in a slot can be compared at all; whether a new player has a default.
- **How much to remember?** Count, for a character at each Stage: buttons, always-on rule changes, conditional or off-turn triggers. The third count is the one to keep near zero.
- **How many must be written?** Slots × options per slot × levels, against Street's ceiling and the filler test.
- **What does a mistake cost?** How a regretted pick is undone, and whether free swapping lets the table demand a swap.
- **Does the list eat the base rules?** Every new option needs ground; check where it comes from.
- **Does the grid stay fixed?** What happens when option number N+1 is added.

### (d) Open disagreements he will have to decide

1. **Balance of options or a slope.** Sawyer and Introcaso against Alexander and Rosewater.
2. **Options as the center or as the flourish.** Abadia, Mearls and 5e say the flourish; Lucas has said the meat. Draw Steel and Daggerheart put the meat in class abilities and domain cards, chosen from a small hand.
3. **A hard wall between combat and non-combat, or options that serve both.** Silos against "an artificial, metagame distinction".
4. **Reliability outside a fight.** The loved non-combat options are certain or have a floor; `roleplay-skills.md` names certainty a failure mode. How certain is too certain is his line to draw (Routine is one answer already taken).
5. **Free swapping or weight.** Experimentation and no regret, against a choice that means something and a table that can't demand a respec.
6. **A hand or a catalogue; minor and major or one size; ranks or new verbs.**
7. **Should a pick be allowed to trade fighting for something else at all** ("They want the ability to make that trade-off").
8. **Numbers in Talents.** Baker's test against the need to raise a skill by something.
